# Phase 01 — Andragogy & the Self-Directing Adult

**Part:** I — Adult Learning Theory
**Applies to:** [Universal]
**Type:** Research
**Status:** Researched — June 2026

---

## Bottom Line

The "self-directing adult learner" is the organizing idea behind three linked frameworks — andragogy (Knowles), self-directed learning (Tough, Garrison, Grow), and heutagogy (Hase & Kenyon). None is a validated theory in the strict sense; all are best treated as sets of assumptions and design heuristics with thin, mixed empirical support. The single most important practical finding is that **learner autonomy is a developmental achievement, not a starting assumption**: novices generally need structure, modeling, and feedback, and over-relying on self-direction backfires for beginners. Use these frameworks as a staged continuum — direct guidance first, fading to autonomy as competence and confidence grow.

---

## Core Principles

- **Andragogy is assumptions, not a theory.** Knowles built it on experience and observation, not experiments; he himself called it a "system of concepts," not a theory, and later reframed pedagogy/andragogy as a continuum rather than a binary.
- **Self-direction is both a process and a goal.** SDL describes *how* learning is organized (learner takes initiative) and *what* we hope to cultivate (a self-directing disposition).
- **Heutagogy extends SDL to learner-determined goals** (capability, double-loop learning, non-linear paths) — and has the weakest evidence of the three.
- **Autonomy presupposes prior knowledge the novice lacks.** This is the load-bearing caution; it connects directly to cognitive load and the expertise-reversal effect (Phase 05).
- **Match teaching style to learner readiness, per domain.** An experienced adult can be a rank novice in a new field and should be taught accordingly (Grow).

## Deep Dive

### Andragogy

Knowles defined andragogy as "the art and science of helping adults learn." The term has a long European lineage predating him: Kapp coined *Andragogik* in 1833; Lindeman (a colleague of Dewey) introduced it into English in 1926, emphasizing the informal, experiential, lifelong, civic nature of adult learning. Knowles brought it to wide attention from 1968.

**Knowles's six assumptions about adult learners:** (1) *self-concept* — adults move from dependent toward self-directing; (2) *experience* — a growing reservoir that is a resource for learning; (3) *readiness* — oriented to developmental tasks of social roles; (4) *orientation* — problem-centered, seeking immediate application; (5) *motivation* — increasingly internal; (6) *the need to know* — adults need to know *why* before investing. (Knowles published four assumptions in 1970/1980, added motivation in 1984; "need to know" is usually counted sixth.)

**Continuum, not dichotomy.** Knowles initially opposed andragogy to pedagogy, then explicitly retreated. In "Andragogy Revisited Part II" (1979) he called the "versus" framing a mistake — it should read "From Pedagogy to Andragogy," with the assumptions on a continuum. In 1980 he described the models as "two ends of a spectrum, with a realistic assumption in a given situation falling in between." This concession is decisive.

### Self-Directed Learning (SDL)

Knowles (1975) defined SDL as a process in which individuals "take the initiative … in diagnosing their learning needs, formulating learning goals, identifying … resources, choosing and implementing appropriate learning strategies, and evaluating learning outcomes."

- **Tough (1971)** documented the ubiquity of self-planned learning (commonly cited: ~98% of adults engage in learning projects, ~70%+ self-planned) — but measured *prevalence*, never *effectiveness*, a limitation often forgotten when his work is cited as proof SDL "works."
- **Garrison's comprehensive model (1997)** integrates three interacting dimensions: *self-management* (external task control), *self-monitoring* (cognitive/metacognitive responsibility), and *motivation* (entering + task motivation).
- **Grow's Staged Self-Directed Learning (1991)** adapts Situational Leadership: match teacher style to learner readiness and move the learner toward self-direction. Four stages: **S1 Dependent** → teacher as authority/coach (direct instruction, drills, feedback); **S2 Interested** → motivator/guide; **S3 Involved** → facilitator; **S4 Self-directed** → consultant/delegator. The most damaging mismatch is a dependent learner (S1) with a non-directive teacher (T4). Grow stressed self-direction is *situational* — one can be S4 in one domain and S1 in another.
- **Measurement:** Guglielmino's Self-Directed Learning Readiness Scale (SDLRS, 1977) is the most-used instrument and the most criticized — Field (1989, 1991) found it "structurally unsound," likely measuring love-of-learning rather than readiness for *self-directed* learning; the eight-factor structure repeatedly fails to replicate.

### Heutagogy

Hase & Kenyon (2000) coined heutagogy — self-*determined* learning, where the learner sets the goals and the path (vs. SDL, where the learner directs process within often externally-set goals). Key concepts: *learner agency*; *double-loop learning* (reflecting on values and on how one learns); *capability* (confident application to novel situations, beyond demonstrated competence); and *non-linear, learner-defined paths*. Usually positioned as the third stage of a **pedagogy → andragogy → heutagogy continuum**, which itself concedes the early (novice) stage is appropriately teacher-directed.

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Andragogy — weak empirical base.** Bowlby (2007): "Knowles' andragogical assumptions were not formulated on empirical research." Rachal (1994) found "approximate equivalence" between andragogical and pedagogical approaches on achievement and satisfaction; Rachal (2002) catalogued why the constructs resist rigorous testing. Hartree (1984) named three core problems (theory of teaching vs. learning; the adult–child relationship; theory vs. practice).
- **Cultural critique.** Andragogy presupposes an individualist, white, middle-class norm (Pratt 1993; Flannery 1994; Johnson-Bailey & Cervero 2000; Brookfield 2014), marginalizing collectivist learning cultures and learners differing by race/gender.
- **The adult/child boundary collapses** (Merriam 2001): some adults are highly dependent; some children are independent self-directed learners.
- **SDL — best evidence is in health professions.** Murad et al. (2010, *Medical Education*; 59 studies, 8,011 learners): SDL gave a *moderate* improvement in knowledge (SMD 0.45) but a *trivial, non-significant* effect on skills (SMD 0.05); it worked better when learners chose their own resources, and **advanced learners benefited more than novices**. The 2025 update (Maggio, Martin et al., *Perspectives on Medical Education*) reaffirmed small-to-moderate knowledge gains, modest skill/behavior gains, wide variability in how "SDL" is defined, and a (non-significant) trend favoring structured facilitator support.
- **Brookfield's critique (1984, 2000):** SDL research over-relies on middle-class samples and measures of *extent* not *quality*, neglects social context, and romanticizes the "intellectual Robinson Crusoe" (most SDL is socially embedded).
- **Heutagogy — weakest evidence.** Agonács & Matos (2019) found researchers mostly *understanding* rather than *explaining/predicting* heutagogy; later reviews found only ~21 empirical studies, mostly descriptive. Kirschner and colleagues call the whole PAH distinction "flawed" because an adult can be just as much a novice as a child in a new area.

**The central, well-evidenced tension — autonomy backfires for novices.** Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006, *Educational Psychologist*) argue from cognitive architecture that minimally guided instruction is "less effective and less efficient" for novices, whose advantage from guidance "recedes only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge." The expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003; Kalyuga 2007) shows supports that help novices become redundant or harmful for experts (and vice versa) — implying guidance fading. Ericsson's deliberate-practice work (1993) argues expertise requires structured, feedback-rich practice typically designed by a teacher — though this "teacher necessary" claim is contested (Macnamara & Maitra 2019 found a smaller effect and rated solitary practice *more* relevant than teacher-designed).

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Reserve SDL and individualized learning plans for senior trainees and practitioners (Murad: advanced learners benefit more; SDL helps knowledge more than skills); scaffold juniors with direct instruction and supervised practice. New hires/residents should not be thrown into pure SDL on unfamiliar, high-stakes skills; gate self-direction to later training years. Procedural skills require expert-supervised deliberate practice, not self-guided trial and error.

### Ministry / Teaching
Match teaching style to readiness: catechesis and foundational doctrine for newcomers benefit from structured, authoritative teaching; mature members thrive in self-chosen study, learning covenants, and lay-led ministry. Watch for the S1-learner / T4-teacher mismatch — common in volunteer-led small groups that assume everyone is ready to self-direct.

### Creative / Content
Self-planned learning projects and self-determined goals (heutagogy) are where creative voice flourishes — but only once foundations and feedback loops exist. Beginners left to pure self-direction ingrain bad habits. Online tutorials and creator communities provide modeling but typically lack the reciprocal feedback of a true community of practice — the key weakness of purely self-taught online creators.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Diagnose stage, not age.** Assess Grow's S1–S4 readiness *per domain* before choosing a teaching style.
- **Structure first, fade to autonomy.** Direct instruction for novices; release responsibility as competence grows.
- **Gate full SDL/heutagogy to demonstrated readiness** (relevant prior knowledge + metacognitive skill + intrinsic motivation).
- **Never remove the facilitator entirely** — even self-directed designs should embed feedback and support.
- **Don't use the Guglielmino SDLRS as a high-stakes gate** given its contested validity; triangulate with behavioral evidence.
- **Honor the evidence asymmetry:** the strongest finding here is *against* unguided autonomy for novices.
