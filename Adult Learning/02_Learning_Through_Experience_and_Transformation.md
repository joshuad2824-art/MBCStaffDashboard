**Part:** I — Adult Learning Theory
**Applies to:** [Universal]
**Type:** Research
**Status:** Researched — June 2026

---

## Bottom Line

The experiential learning **cycle** (Kolb's four stages, rooted in Dewey, Lewin, and Piaget) and **reflective practice** (Schön, Gibbs, Boud) are durable, widely adopted process models — but the **learning STYLES** built on Kolb's cycle are largely discredited (Coffield 2004; Pashler 2008 found no credible evidence for matching instruction to styles). **Transformative learning** (Mezirow) offers a powerful account of deep perspective change but is hard to measure, over-weights rationality, under-theorizes power, and raises ethical questions about deliberately inducing "disorienting dilemmas." The common throughline is Dewey's warning that **not all experience is educative**: experience produces learning only with skilled facilitation, scaffolding for novices, and structured reflection.

---

## Core Principles

- **Adopt the cycle, drop the styles.** Experience → reflection → conceptualization → application is a useful design heuristic; matching teaching to "learning styles" is not supported.
- **Reflection is theoretically rich but empirically thin** — and must be structured and supported (especially for novices), not mandated as graded compliance.
- **Deep learning can reframe a whole perspective** (transformative learning), but the change is largely emotional/relational as well as rational, and cannot be forced.
- **Not all experience is educative** (Dewey): experience becomes learning only through skilled facilitation and guided reflection.
- **Experience and reflection both need scaffolding for novices** — the same novice/structure logic as the rest of Part I.

## Deep Dive

### Experiential Learning Theory

Kolb defined learning as "the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience." The four-stage cycle: **Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualization → Active Experimentation**, organized as two dialectics (grasping: CE↔AC; transforming: RO↔AE). Six propositions frame ELT (learning is a process; all learning is relearning; it requires resolving dialectical conflict; it is holistic; it is transactional with the environment; it creates knowledge).

**Roots Kolb named:** Dewey (*Experience and Education*, 1938 — experience is foundational but *not all experience is educative*; the criteria are **continuity** and **interaction**, and a "mis-educative" experience "arrests or distorts the growth of further experience"); Lewin (action research, T-groups, the engineering concept of *feedback*); and Piaget.

**The styles, and why they're contested.** Kolb derived four styles (Diverging, Assimilating, Converging, Accommodating) measured by the Learning Style Inventory (now KLSI 4.0). The LSI uses an ipsative forced-choice format that creates artifactual correlations; internal consistency is acceptable but test-retest reliability is poor and construct validity inconclusive. Coffield et al. (2004) reviewed 13 influential models (including Kolb's) and found serious reliability/validity weaknesses. Pashler et al. (2008) defined the "meshing hypothesis" (teach to a learner's style) and showed the crossover interaction needed to validate it is almost never found; where adequate methods were used, results often *contradicted* meshing. **Distinguish sharply:** the *cycle* is a defensible heuristic (though critiqued as over-linear by Bergsteiner et al. 2010); the *styles* and teaching-to-them are not supported.

### Reflection and Reflective Practice

Schön critiqued "technical rationality" (the positivist view that practice is just applying theory) and located real practice in the "swampy lowlands" of messy problems. His distinctions: *knowing-in-action* (tacit know-how), *reflection-in-action* (thinking on your feet, triggered by surprise), and *reflection-on-action* (retrospective). His paradigm case was the architectural design studio — design as a "reflective conversation with the situation." Argyris & Schön added single-loop (correcting within existing goals) vs **double-loop** (questioning the governing variables) learning.

**Structured models:** Gibbs' cycle (Description → Feelings → Evaluation → Analysis → Conclusion → Action Plan — notable for including feelings); Borton/Rolfe's "What? So what? Now what?"; Boud et al. (return to the experience, attend to feelings, re-evaluate).

### Transformative Learning

Mezirow's constructs: **frames of reference** (structures of assumptions) made of durable *habits of mind* and changeable *points of view*; the **disorienting dilemma** that triggers change; the **~10 phases** of perspective transformation (from the dilemma through self-examination, critical assessment of assumptions, exploration of new roles, to reintegration); and the centrality of **critical self-reflection** and **rational discourse** (drawing on Habermas).

**Other strands:** Boyd's Jungian *individuation* (emotion, imagination, symbol); Daloz's developmental mentoring (support + challenge); Freire's social-emancipatory *conscientização* (critical consciousness, problem-posing vs the "banking model," praxis). Later scholarship (Taylor, Dirkx) argues Mezirow under-weighted emotion, intuition, relationship, and the body.

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Cycle vs styles:** the cycle is a useful process heuristic; the styles are essentially refuted (Coffield 2004; Pashler 2008). This is a genuine, important myth-correction.
- **Reflection — weak outcome evidence.** The construct is vague and over-proliferated; research is dominated by small samples, single iterations, and self-report; there is little robust causal evidence that reflection improves outcomes. It is hard to teach and assess, and mandating/grading it risks "performative" compliance (Hobbs 2007; Galea 2012; Brookfield's "zombie reflection").
- **Transformative learning — mostly qualitative.** Taylor's reviews (1997, 1998, 2007 in *International Journal of Lifelong Education*; 40 peer-reviewed studies in the 2007 update) found TL "the most researched and discussed theory in adult education," with support but heavy reliance on retrospective interview self-report; perspective transformation is hard to operationalize. Common critiques: over-emphasis on rationality (Boyd & Myers 1988); under-theorized context and power (Tennant; Clark & Wilson 1991); and ethical concerns about deliberately provoking disorienting dilemmas given the facilitator's power.
- **The unifying caveat:** experience needs facilitation and reflection needs scaffolding — mirroring Kirschner/Sweller/Clark (2006) on minimally guided instruction. The resolution relocates structure from "transmitting content" to "designing experiences, sequencing challenge, and guiding reflection."

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Simulation + **guided debrief** is the flagship — the large effects of simulation depend on the quality of the facilitated debrief, not the scenario. Cook et al. (2011, *JAMA*; 609 studies, 35,226 trainees) found pooled effects ~1.20 (knowledge) and ~1.09 (process skills) vs no intervention. Cheng et al. (2013, *JAMA Pediatrics*, EXPRESS trial) found *scripted* debriefing produced greater knowledge and team-leader gains. Treat reflective portfolios as developmental, not pure assessment (the performativity risk). Invest in debriefer/faculty training and psychological safety.

### Ministry / Teaching
Faith formation maps onto the cycle and resonates powerfully with conversion/transformation language — which is exactly why *ethical care* is paramount given the authority gradient. Favor relational mentoring (Daloz: support + challenge) and voluntary discourse over engineered crisis. Boyd's depth-psychology and Daloz's mentoring strands fit identity/faith work better than a purely rationalist program.

### Creative / Content
Craft is learned through **make → critique → reframe → remake** loops (Kolb's cycle; Schön's studio "reflective conversation"). Use deliberate practice for technical sub-skills but protect *exploratory* practice for voice (Shimizu & Okada 2018 on breakdancers' exploratory practice). Major creative breakthroughs are often emotionally driven reframings — Boyd/Dirkx fits better than rational Mezirow. Garnett's heutagogical reading of the Beatles (pedagogy under George Martin → andragogy among peers → heutagogy treating the studio as an instrument) captures the developmental sequence.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Use the cycle; never assess or teach to "learning styles."**
- **Structure reflection** with explicit frames (Gibbs; "What/So what/Now what") and expert coaching for novices; fade as competence grows.
- **Aim for double-loop reflection** (questioning assumptions), not mere description.
- **Don't manufacture traumatic "disorienting dilemmas,"** especially from a position of authority.
- **Separate developmental reflection from high-stakes grading** to reduce "faking."
- **The facilitator/debriefer is the lever** — the single highest-leverage investment.
