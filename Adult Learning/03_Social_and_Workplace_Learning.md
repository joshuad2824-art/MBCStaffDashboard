## Bottom Line

Much adult learning is **social and situated** — it happens through participation in a community, not just transmission into an individual head. Situated learning / legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger) and communities of practice (Wenger) reframed learning as becoming a member of a practice; action learning (Revans) operationalizes "learn by solving real problems together"; Bandura's social learning theory supplies the well-evidenced individual mechanism (observational learning, self-efficacy). The honest caveat: the situated/CoP tradition is theoretically influential but **empirically hard to test and weakly standardized**, and its later commercialized form drifts from a descriptive insight ("this is how learning happens") into a prescriptive management tool ("engineer this for knowledge management") that critics say loses its critical edge. The strongest empirical anchor here is Bandura.

---

## Core Principles

- **Learning is participation, not just acquisition.** Becoming competent means moving from the periphery toward full membership in a community of practice.
- **Knowledge is situated and partly tacit/distributed** — embedded in activity, tools, relationships, and context, not only in individual minds.
- **You learn by watching and modeling** (Bandura): attention → retention → reproduction → motivation, all amplified by self-efficacy.
- **Real problems + a questioning group + reflection = learning** (Revans's L = P + Q).
- **Communities of practice can be cultivated but not fully managed** — the descriptive-to-prescriptive turn is exactly where the evidence thins and the critiques bite.

## Deep Dive

### Situated Learning & Legitimate Peripheral Participation (LPP)

Lave & Wenger (1991) reframed learning as an aspect of *participation in social practice* rather than internalization of knowledge by an individual. Their apprenticeship studies (Yucatec midwives, Vai and Gola tailors, U.S. Navy quartermasters, meat cutters, non-drinking alcoholics in AA) showed newcomers entering at the **periphery** with **legitimate** but limited real tasks, then moving toward **full participation** as "old-timers." Three features of the concept: it is *legitimate* (the newcomer genuinely belongs), *peripheral* (low-risk, partial), and *participation* (engaged in the actual practice). Learning is inseparable from **identity** ("becoming a tailor," not just "learning tailoring") and cognition is treated as **distributed** across people, tools, and setting.

### Communities of Practice (CoP)

Wenger (1998) generalized the insight into the community of practice — "groups of people who share a concern or a passion for something they do and learn how to do it better as they interact regularly." His three constitutive dimensions: **mutual engagement** (people doing things together), **joint enterprise** (a shared, negotiated purpose), and **shared repertoire** (common resources — language, routines, tools, stories). Identity and meaning are central.

**The managerial turn.** Wenger, McDermott & Snyder (2002) recast CoPs as cultivable knowledge-management assets defined by three elements — **domain** (the area of shared interest), **community** (relationships and trust), and **practice** (the shared body of knowledge/methods) — with seven cultivation principles and a five-stage lifecycle (potential → coalescing → maturing → stewardship → transformation) and a named **community coordinator/steward** role. This shift from *describing* how learning naturally occurs to *prescribing* how to engineer it is both the source of CoP's organizational popularity and the focus of its sharpest critiques.

### Action Learning

Reg Revans (originator, from work with the UK National Coal Board and hospitals) built action learning on the equation **L = P + Q**: learning equals *programmed knowledge* (existing expertise, the content of textbooks) plus *questioning insight* (fresh questions asked of real problems). His maxim: the rate of learning must be at least equal to the rate of change. Practitioners work in small **sets** on real, significant problems they own, alternating action with reflection. Marquardt (2004) codified six components: a **problem**, a **group/set** (diverse, ~4–8), a **questioning-and-reflection process**, **taking action**, a **commitment to learning**, and a **facilitator/coach (set adviser)** who protects the process rather than solving the problem.

### Adjacent theories: social learning and connectivism

- **Bandura's social/social-cognitive learning** supplies the individual mechanism. Observational learning runs through four sub-processes — **attention, retention, reproduction, motivation** — and behavior, person, and environment shape each other (*reciprocal determinism*). The Bobo doll studies (1961–63) demonstrated modeling of behavior. **Self-efficacy** (belief in one's capability) became one of the most influential and best-evidenced constructs in psychology. This is the empirical anchor of the phase.
- **Connectivism** (Siemens & Downes, 2005) proposes that in a digital, networked age, learning is the capacity to form and traverse networks — knowledge is distributed across people and technology, and "know-where" (where to find knowledge) rivals "know-what" and "know-how." It birthed the early MOOCs. Its status is **contested**: critics (Verhagen; Kop & Hill, 2008, *IRRODL*, "Connectivism: Learning theory of the future or vestige of the past?") argue it is better understood as a pedagogical perspective than a stand-alone learning theory.

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Bandura — strong.** Observational learning and self-efficacy are robustly supported across decades and domains; self-efficacy reliably predicts effort, persistence, and performance.
- **Situated learning / CoP — influential but hard to test.** Ranmuthugala et al. (2011) systematically reviewed how and why CoPs work in healthcare and found enormous heterogeneity in definition, structure, and purpose, weak standardization, and limited rigorous outcome evidence — CoPs are plausibly valuable but under-evidenced as a measurable intervention.
- **Action learning — practitioner-strong, controlled-evidence-thin.** Leonard & Marquardt (2010) and later reviews find broad practitioner endorsement and rich case evidence but few controlled studies isolating its effects.
- **Connectivism — contested as a theory** (see above).
- **Critiques of the situated/CoP tradition:**
  - *Fox (2000)* applies actor-network theory to argue CoP under-theorizes how practices and innovations actually spread.
  - *Contu & Willmott (2003)* argue that commercializing CoP for knowledge management strips out the original theory's attention to **power, conflict, and inequality** — domesticating a radical insight into a benign management tool.
  - *Roberts (2006)* catalogs the limits of CoP: power relations, trust, members' predisposition, group size, and spatial/organizational reach all constrain when CoPs work.
  - LPP can **romanticize apprenticeship**, downplaying exploitation, gatekeeping, exclusion, and conflict at the periphery.
  - The **descriptive→prescriptive drift**: a community that arises organically may resist being engineered on demand.

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Residency and clinical rotations *are* legitimate peripheral participation — design onboarding explicitly as movement from the periphery (observation, low-risk tasks) toward full participation, with graduated responsibility. Cultivate clinician communities of practice (e.g., informatics super-user groups, specialty user forums) for tacit-knowledge transfer that documentation can't carry. Use **action learning sets** for quality-improvement and informatics-adoption problems: a real problem, a diverse set, a skilled set adviser. Recognize much ward learning is observational (Bandura) — model the behavior you want, visibly.

### Ministry / Teaching
Discipleship is apprenticeship: the church functions as a community of practice with its own joint enterprise and shared repertoire. Give youth (grades 7–12) **legitimate peripheral** roles — real, bounded responsibilities that signal genuine belonging — and move them toward fuller participation over time. Lean on modeling (Bandura): faith is caught as much as taught, and self-efficacy ("I can serve / lead / share") is built by guided, successful participation. Mentoring pairs and small groups are the structural unit.

### Creative / Content
Craft is learned by participating in a community — guilds, critique groups, creator collectives — not only by solo tutorials, which is the structural weakness of the purely self-taught online creator (modeling without reciprocal feedback). For Timber & Ink, **stewarding** a creator community (Wenger's coordinator role; cultivation principles; rhythm) turns an audience into a community of practice. Connectivism is the honest description of how creators actually learn online (networks, "know-where"), held with appropriate skepticism about its theoretical status.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Design onboarding as periphery → full participation,** with legitimate real tasks from day one.
- **Make the implicit visible:** model the practice; new members learn by watching old-timers.
- **For wicked problems, form a set** (real problem + diverse group + skilled set adviser + reflection), not a course.
- **Cultivate, don't command, communities** — provide domain, relationships, rhythm, and a steward; expect organic growth.
- **Build self-efficacy through guided early wins** (Bandura), especially for novices and youth.
- **Watch for power and exclusion** at the periphery; legitimacy must be real, not nominal.
