---

## Bottom Line

Three convergent cognitive-science frameworks — Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), and Paivio's Dual Coding Theory (DCT) — agree that working memory is severely limited and long-term memory is vast, so instructional materials should strip out extraneous load, present words and pictures together in mutually reinforcing (non-redundant) ways, and give heavy guidance to novices. The evidence is real and replicated but the effect sizes are inflated by short lab studies, college samples, immediate post-tests, and publication bias. The single most decision-relevant finding is the **expertise-reversal effect**: load-reducing supports that strongly help novices become useless or actively harmful as learners gain expertise — the cognitive mechanism beneath "structure first, fade to autonomy."

---

## Core Principles

- **Working memory is the bottleneck; long-term memory is effectively unlimited.** Learning = building and automating schemas, which collapse many elements into one working-memory slot.
- **Cut extraneous load; manage intrinsic load; the "germane" category has been folded into intrinsic.** The current theory recognizes two sources of load, not three.
- **Mayer's principles are design rules with the field's most quantified evidence** — but treat the headline effect sizes as upper bounds.
- **Dual coding is universal, not a "learning style."** Combining well-integrated words and visuals helps everyone; matching people to a single channel does not.
- **Match the design to expertise and fade the support.** What helps novices harms experts (expertise reversal); build a scaffolded version and a quick-reference version.

## Deep Dive

### Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)

**Origins.** Sweller developed CLT in the 1980s out of problem-solving research at UNSW. Studying learners solving algebra/geometry problems, he found that conventional problem solving via **means-ends analysis** (Newell & Simon 1972) consumes so much working memory that little is left for schema acquisition — learners solve the problem but don't learn its structure. Foundational papers: Sweller & Cooper (1985); Sweller (1988).

**Cognitive architecture.** CLT assumes (a) a limited working memory for novel information — Miller's (1956) "seven plus or minus two," revised down to ~4 chunks by Cowan (2001); (b) Baddeley's separate phonological and visuospatial components; (c) an effectively unlimited long-term memory of schemas. Working memory has no known limits when drawing on stored schemas — which is why expertise dissolves the bottleneck.

**Evolutionary account.** Drawing on Geary, Sweller separates **biologically primary** knowledge (first language, general problem solving — acquired easily, without instruction) from **biologically secondary** knowledge (reading, mathematics — effortful, requiring explicit instruction). CLT applies to secondary knowledge. The 2019 review frames five principles, including the **borrowing-and-reorganising principle** (we acquire most knowledge from others, not by discovery) and the **narrow-limits-of-change principle** — the theoretical justification for direct, explicit instruction over discovery learning (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006).

**The three loads and the germane-load debate.** Intrinsic load is set by **element interactivity** (how many elements must be processed simultaneously) relative to prior knowledge. Extraneous load comes from poor design. Germane load was originally (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 1998) a third additive type (resources spent building schemas). The reconceptualization (Sweller 2010; Kalyuga 2011, "How many types of load does it really need?") folds germane into intrinsic: **two sources of load (intrinsic + extraneous) compete for working memory; "germane resources" are simply the portion allocated to dealing with intrinsic load.** Many secondary sources still teach the obsolete three-additive-types model.

**Major CLT effects (with seminal studies):**
- **Worked example effect** (Sweller & Cooper 1985): studying worked examples beats unguided problem solving for novices. Independent meta-analysis on mathematics (Barbieri et al. 2023, *Educational Psychology Review*): pooled g = 0.48 across 55 studies / 181 effect sizes.
- **Split-attention effect** (Tarmizi & Sweller 1988; Chandler & Sweller 1992): integrate diagram and explanatory text physically. Meta-analytically ~g = 0.63–0.72 (Schroeder & Cenkci 2018; Ginns 2006), larger for complex material.
- **Redundancy effect** (Chandler & Sweller 1991): presenting the same self-sufficient information in two forms (e.g., narration plus identical on-screen text) *harms* learning. Distinct from split attention (which integrates mutually *dependent* sources).
- **Modality effect** (Mousavi, Low & Sweller 1995; Tindall-Ford et al. 1997): words as speech rather than on-screen text frees the visual channel — heavily caveated (see below).
- **Goal-free, completion-problem, and element-interactivity effects** (Sweller, Mawer & Ward 1983; van Merriënboer & Krammer 1987; Sweller 1994).
- **Expertise-reversal effect** (Kalyuga et al. 2003; Kalyuga 2007) and the **guidance-fading effect** — see Evidence Base.

### Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)

**Three assumptions:** dual channels (auditory/verbal and visual/pictorial, built on Paivio and Baddeley); limited capacity per channel; active processing. The **SOI model**: learners **S**elect relevant words/images, **O**rganize them into verbal and pictorial models, and **I**ntegrate those with prior knowledge. Mayer reframes load as three demands: extraneous, essential (intrinsic), and generative processing.

**The 15 principles, by function, with Mayer's cited median effect sizes and box-score support** (Mayer 2021; *Cambridge Handbook*; figures vary slightly across editions):

*Reducing extraneous processing:* **Coherence** (cut extraneous material/"seductive details") d ≈ 0.86; **Signaling/cueing** d ≈ 0.41–0.46 (weaker, variable); **Redundancy** (don't duplicate narration as on-screen text) d ≈ 0.86–0.87; **Spatial contiguity** (words next to graphics) d ≈ 0.79; **Temporal contiguity** (narration and animation simultaneous) d ≈ 1.22–1.30 (one of the largest).

*Managing essential processing:* **Segmenting** (learner-paced chunks) d ≈ 0.70–0.79; **Pre-training** (teach key names/concepts first) d ≈ 0.75; **Modality** (spoken rather than printed words with graphics) d ≈ 0.76 (up to ≈ 1.02 in earlier sets).

*Fostering generative processing (largely social-cue principles):* **Multimedia** (words + pictures beat words alone) d ≈ 1.5–1.67 in Mayer's small set (independent estimates far more modest); **Personalization** (conversational style) d ≈ 0.79; **Voice** (human over machine) d ≈ 0.74; **Image** (adding the speaker's face) d ≈ 0.20 — explicitly negligible; **Embodiment** (gesturing agents) d ≈ 0.36; **Generative activity** (summarizing, drawing, self-testing) supported (Fiorella & Mayer 2015); **Immersion** (3D/VR) does *not* necessarily beat 2D.

### Dual Coding Theory (DCT)

Paivio's DCT posits two functionally independent but interconnected systems: a **verbal system** (logogens, sequential) and a **nonverbal/imagery system** (imagens, parallel), with representational, referential, and associative processing. The **picture superiority effect** and **concreteness effect** are core pillars: concrete material can be dual-coded, giving two retrieval routes. DCT is the parent of CTML's dual-channels assumption and the explanatory basis for CLT's modality effect. It is **not** the "visual learners" myth — dual coding says combining channels helps *everyone*; learning styles (refuted by Pashler et al. 2008) claims individuals should be matched to one channel. A theoretical rival exists (propositional/single-code accounts, e.g., Pylyshyn).

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Overall multimedia:** Noetel et al. (2022, *Review of Educational Research*; 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants) found well-designed multimedia beats non-multimedia at g = 0.51 (procedural g = 0.73; conceptual g = 0.62) — supporting the principles "only when done right."
- **Modality is the cautionary tale:** Ginns (2005) found d ≈ 0.72 but with massive moderation (system-paced d = 0.93 vs. self-paced d = −0.14). Reinwein (2012) found d = 0.38, dropping **to d = 0.20 after publication-bias correction** — roughly a quarter of Mayer's headline figure.
- **Redundancy:** Adesope & Nesbit (2012) found fully verbatim redundant text adds essentially nothing over text alone (g ≈ −0.04); the benefit of adding written to spoken words holds mainly for low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced materials, and picture-free content.
- **Coherence/seductive details:** independent estimates are smaller than Mayer's 0.86 (Rey 2012; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, g ≈ −0.33; a multilevel re-analysis shrinks it to ~−0.16).
- **The expertise-reversal effect — the central design lever.** Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025, *Learning and Instruction*; 60 studies, 176 effect sizes, 5,924 participants): low-prior-knowledge learners learn better from high-assistance instruction (d = 0.505) while high-prior-knowledge learners learn better from low-assistance instruction (d = −0.428). The effect is **asymmetric** — "providing novices with assistance has a stronger effect than withholding assistance from experts." The corollary is the **guidance-fading effect** (worked examples → completion problems → full problems), operationalized in medical education (van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010, *Medical Education*; AMEE Guide No. 86, 2014).
- **Major program-level limitations:**
  - *Measurement.* The loads can't be cleanly measured independently; the dominant instrument is Paas's (1992) single-item mental-effort scale (good for overall load, not for separating types). Sweller conceded load was originally "an assumed rather than a measured construct."
  - *Falsifiability.* CLT is criticized (Gerjets et al. 2009; de Jong 2010) as hard to falsify because load is inferred from the outcome it explains. Sweller (2023) reframes replication failures as productive theory expansion — adaptive, but it underscores the worry.
  - *Publication bias.* Ropovik et al. (2021, *PLOS One*): ~800 meta-analyses average d = .40, while pre-registered large RCTs average d ≈ .06, and multi-lab replications run about a third the size of matching meta-analyses. Treat CLT/multimedia effects as upper bounds.
  - *Ecological validity.* Canonical studies are short (often <30 min), undergraduate, artificial materials, immediate post-tests.
  - *Reconceptualizations.* Germane load redefined (2010–2011); evolutionary principles added (2006–2019) — older studies operationalized constructs the current theory has abandoned.

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Clinicians span a wide expertise range, so build tiered artifacts. A fully scaffolded onboarding walkthrough (annotated screenshots with callouts placed directly on the UI element — spatial contiguity; one task per segment — segmenting; key terms pre-taught) should fade into a one-page EHR quick-reference for experienced users. Avoid redundancy (don't caption a self-explanatory screenshot with text that restates it); cut institutional boilerplate from task-critical aids (coherence). High intrinsic load in clinical tasks (many interacting elements, often under time pressure) leaves little spare capacity, so extraneous load must be near zero. CLT is explicitly endorsed in medical-education guidance (van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010; AMEE Guide No. 86).

### Ministry / Teaching
For presenter-paced lessons and sermons-with-slides: narrate over visuals with key terms only, never read bullet text verbatim (redundancy + modality); put labels on any diagram (spatial contiguity); reveal complex builds in segments; cut decorative slides (coherence); pre-teach key terms. For learner-paced handouts (self-study, small-group materials), keep explanatory text on the page — the modality/redundancy advantages largely vanish under self-pacing. Pair text with explanatory (not decorative) visuals.

### Creative / Content
Slides/decks: one idea per visual unit; cut decorative images, background music, and tangential "fun facts"; use a conversational register (personalization). For tutorials and longer guides (learner-paced), on-page explanatory text is appropriate; lead with worked examples and fade to independent practice. Quick-reference cards for an already-skilled audience are the *faded* end state — strip scaffolding, use clean tables/icons paired with terse text. On camera/podcast, a friendly human voice beats a machine voice (voice principle); adding the talking-head face adds little (image principle ≈ 0.20).

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **One idea per visual unit;** cut anything decorative or tangential (coherence).
- **Labels on the graphic, never in a separate legend** (anti-split-attention).
- **Never display text you'll read aloud verbatim** (redundancy); narrate over visuals with key terms only for presenter-paced media (modality).
- **Segment complex content; pre-teach vocabulary.**
- **Build two versions:** a novice "full-scaffold" and an expert "quick-reference"; route by expertise.
- **Pace-aware channel choice:** spoken-narration design for presenter-paced; keep on-page text for learner-paced.
- **Treat published effect sizes as upper bounds;** pilot with your real users and measure transfer/delayed retention.
