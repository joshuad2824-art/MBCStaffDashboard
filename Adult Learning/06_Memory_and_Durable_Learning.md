# Phase 06 — Memory & Durable Learning

**Part:** II — The Cognitive Science of Learning
**Applies to:** [Universal]
**Type:** Research
**Status:** Researched — June 2026

---

## Bottom Line

The single most robust prescription in the science of learning is to replace passive review (rereading, highlighting) with **effortful retrieval, spread out over time**: practice testing and distributed practice are the only two of ten common study techniques that Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated "high utility," and both rest on hundreds of replications and multiple meta-analyses (testing effect g ≈ 0.50–0.61; spacing among the most replicated findings in all of psychology). These techniques work precisely *because* they feel harder — Robert Bjork's "desirable difficulties" framework shows that ease and fluency during study are unreliable, often inverse, indicators of long-term retention. This is the deliberate counterweight to Phase 05's goal of making the moment of instruction easy: reduce *extraneous* difficulty, but introduce *desirable* difficulty.

---

## Core Principles

- **Retrieval beats restudy.** The act of recalling is itself the learning event, not just a measurement.
- **Spacing beats massing.** The same total time, distributed, produces far more durable retention than cramming.
- **Interleaving beats blocking — when categories are confusable.** Mixing forces discrimination and strategy selection.
- **Ease in the moment ≠ learning.** Performance during study is an unreliable, sometimes inverse, index of durable learning (Bjork's learning-vs-performance distinction).
- **Difficulty must be *desirable* — surmountable.** Calibrate to expertise: too-hard retrieval for novices fails; scaffold first, then raise difficulty.

## Deep Dive

### The Testing / Retrieval Practice Effect

Retrieving information from memory produces substantially better long-term retention than restudying. Roots: Abbott (1909), Gates (1917), Spitzer (1939). The modern era: **Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*)** — students who repeatedly tested themselves on prose (no feedback) beat restudiers on delayed tests (2 days, 1 week) but lost on a 5-minute test; in Experiment 2 the test-thrice group recalled 61% at one week vs. 40% for the study-four-times group (repeated-study learners forgot 56% of what they knew; repeated-test learners forgot just 13%) — and the restudiers were *more confident*. **Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science*)** — with Swahili–English pairs, repeated *studying* after first success had essentially no effect on one-week recall (~36%) while repeated *testing* produced a large benefit (~80%); students' self-predictions were uncorrelated with outcomes.

**Mechanisms:** retrieval effort (more effortful successful retrievals → greater gains); elaborative retrieval (activating related networks); episodic-context/cue-diagnosticity accounts (Karpicke, Lehman & Aue 2014). Rowland (2014) supports the effortful-processing account (recall beats recognition).

**Feedback** amplifies the effect (Rowland 2014: intermediate testing with feedback g = 0.73 vs. 0.39 without). **Format:** effects appear across recall, recognition, short-answer, and multiple choice; Adesope et al. (2017) found MC practice showed a *larger* weighted effect (0.70) than short-answer (0.48), likely via higher retrieval success and re-exposure. **Transfer:** Butler (2010) and Karpicke & Blunt (2011, *Science*, retrieval beat concept mapping even on inference questions) support transfer — though a 2024 critique flags a confound in the Blunt study; treat the strongest transfer claims with mild caution.

**Strength:** very strong — Phelps (2012), Rowland (2014, g = 0.50), Adesope et al. (2017, g = 0.61 across 272 effects; classroom g ≈ 0.67).

### The Spacing Effect and Distributed Practice

The same total study time produces more durable learning distributed across sessions than massed. Roots: **Ebbinghaus (1885)** documented both the forgetting curve and the efficiency of distributed repetitions; **Murre & Dros (2015, *PLOS One*)** replicated the curve, noting a probable upward jump at ~24 hours (sleep consolidation). **Cepeda et al. (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*)** synthesized 839 assessments from 317 experiments: spacing/lag effects are robust, and inter-study interval and retention interval *jointly* determine retention. **Cepeda et al. (2008, *Psychological Science*)** taught >1,350 people facts with review gaps up to 3.5 months and final tests up to a year out: the optimal gap is roughly **10–20% of the retention interval** (≈20–40% for a 1-week test, falling to ~5–10% for a 1-year test) — e.g., to remember for a year, review roughly monthly.

**Expanding vs. equal intervals:** Landauer & Bjork (1978) proposed expanding; Karpicke & Roediger (2007) found expanding wins at short delays but equal-interval wins long-term, and that the *first* retrieval should be effortful (delayed, not immediate). In practice, *any* spacing beats massing. **Mechanisms:** encoding variability, study-phase retrieval, deficient processing of massed repetitions; formalized in Bjork & Bjork's New Theory of Disuse. **Spaced-repetition systems:** Leitner box (1970s) → SuperMemo/Woźniak (SM-2, late 1980s) → Anki, Memrise, Quizlet, FSRS.

### Interleaving

Mixing different problem types/topics ("interleaved") rather than one type at a time ("blocked") improves retention and especially strategy selection and discrimination. Seminal: Rohrer & Taylor (2007, mixed math problems hurt practice but improved delayed tests); Kornell & Bjork (2008, the paintings/artists studies — interleaving different artists improved inductive transfer, yet most participants believed blocking was better); Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015, classroom RCT). **Mechanism:** the **discriminative-contrast hypothesis** (juxtaposing categories highlights defining differences) plus forcing strategy *choice*, not just execution. **Boundary conditions:** Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin*; 59 studies, 238 effects) found a moderate overall effect (g = 0.42) heavily moderated by **similarity** — interleaving helps most when categories are confusable and can fail or reverse when they're very distinct. It is the most over-generalized of the four; block initial single-skill acquisition, then interleave for discrimination.

### Desirable Difficulties and the Illusions of Fluency

**Bjork (1994)** coined **desirable difficulties**: conditions that slow apparent learning but enhance long-term retention/transfer (spacing, interleaving, testing, varying practice conditions, reducing feedback frequency). The word "desirable" is load-bearing — the difficulty must be surmountable. **Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)**: performance during training is an unreliable, sometimes inverse, index of learning. **Bjork & Bjork (1992) New Theory of Disuse:** distinguishes **storage strength** (durability, only grows) from **retrieval strength** (current accessibility, fluctuates); the lower the retrieval strength when you successfully retrieve, the more storage strength grows — reframing forgetting as a feature. **Metacognitive illusions:** Koriat & Bjork (2005, foresight bias — predicted recall 75.7% vs. actual 60.3%); Kornell & Bjork (students prefer massing/rereading and judge blocking superior even after their own data contradict it) — driven by **processing fluency**.

**Popular synthesis & debunking:** *Make It Stick* (Brown, Roediger & McDaniel 2014); **Dunlosky et al. (2013, *PSPI*)** rated ten techniques — **High utility: practice testing, distributed practice.** Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaving. **Low: summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading** — precisely what students use most.

### Forgetting, Generation, and Elaboration

The Ebbinghaus curve (1885; Murre & Dros 2015) is real — steep early, then flattening — which is why timed retrieval and spaced review must come before the curve bottoms out. **Generation effect** (Slamecka & Graf 1978): self-generated material is remembered better than read material; Bertsch et al. (2007) meta-analysis d = 0.40, rising to ≈0.64 beyond a day. **Self-explanation** (Chi et al.): Bisra et al. (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*) g = 0.55 across 69 effects. **Elaborative interrogation** ("why is this true?") earns moderate utility but its benefit grows with prior knowledge — novices may forge incorrect links, bounding its use for beginners.

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Strength:** these are among the most robust findings in cognitive psychology. Testing g ≈ 0.50–0.61; spacing extremely well-replicated across species/ages/materials; interleaving g = 0.42 (similarity-moderated); generation d = 0.40; self-explanation g = 0.55.
- **The complex-materials / transfer debate (live).** van Gog & Sweller (2015) argued from CLT that the testing effect shrinks or reverses as element interactivity rises (Leahy, Hanham & Sweller 2015 reported a *reverse* effect with timetable tasks). Karpicke & Aue (2015) rebutted: "element interactivity" was never quantified, no worked-example study actually manipulated it, contrary studies were omitted, and the null studies used immediate/massed retrieval of isolated material. Synthesis: retrieval works for complex material *when it is first understood and retrieval is achievable*, but can be attenuated or need scaffolding when learners can't successfully retrieve.
- **Lab-to-classroom gap:** much foundational evidence uses simple verbal materials in single sessions; classroom effects are positive but smaller and noisier (implementation quality matters).
- **Resistance:** because effective techniques feel harder and lower immediate performance, learners and teachers disprefer them (Koriat & Bjork; Kornell & Bjork) — adoption requires teaching the learning-vs-performance distinction.
- **Adherence:** spaced/retrieval systems fail when learners don't keep up (over half of Kerfoot's 2008 medical participants completed fewer than two cycles).
- **Over-testing risk:** heavy summative testing can narrow curricula and induce anxiety; the remedy is frequent *low-stakes formative* retrieval with feedback.
- **Effect-size heterogeneity:** pooled effects mask wide moderation by feedback, format, similarity, retention interval, and expertise — treat them as robust directional guidance, not constants.

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Retrieval practice and spaced education are heavily studied here. B. Price Kerfoot's randomized trials of "spaced education" (e.g., *J Urol* 2007; residents emailed spaced questions over 27 weeks) showed significant retention gains, durable at 2 years (*J Urol* 2009). Anki use independently predicts USMLE Step 1 scores (Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen 2015: each additional ~1,700 unique cards ≈ one more Step 1 point; Wothe et al. 2021: 241 vs. 235). *Actions:* build spaced question banks into onboarding and CME; use cumulative quizzing; interleave differential-diagnosis cases; distribute procedural-skill practice. *Caveats:* not every trial succeeds (Grad et al. 2020 found no significant difference in a Family Medicine RCT), and benefits hinge on adherence.

### Ministry / Teaching
The catechism Q&A format is, structurally, centuries-old retrieval practice with spaced review (Luther's Small Catechism, 1529; the Westminster Shorter Catechism's 107 Q&As). *Actions:* end each lesson/sermon with a few recall questions; send a spaced follow-up days later; use spaced-repetition apps (Memverse, Scripture Typer) for memorization; structure discipleship around recalling and explaining prior material before adding new. **Honest caveat:** there are essentially no rigorous controlled studies *within* religious education — the case rests on extrapolation from general/medical education plus app self-report. Mechanism is sound; direct domain proof is thin.

### Creative / Content
These findings apply to the *knowledge and craft substrate* of creative work (techniques, reference base, retained research), not to creativity itself. *Actions:* keep a spaced-repetition deck of craft techniques, idioms, frameworks, and source facts; practice *generating* (draft from memory, then check sources) rather than re-reading exemplars; interleave skills (editing, structure, sound) once basics are solid; use retrieval-based note review and elaborative interrogation for a writing/podcasting knowledge base. Be realistic: durable retention supports but does not manufacture original output.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Close the source and recall before you check.** One switch (rereading → retrieval) captures most of the gain.
- **Schedule expanding spaced reviews** (~1 day, ~1 week, ~1 month, quarterly); set the first gap at ~10–20% of the target retention interval; automate with a spaced-repetition app.
- **Always give feedback,** ideally slightly delayed, especially for confident errors and MC practice.
- **Interleave confusable categories** once basics are acquired; block the very first acquisition of a single skill.
- **Calibrate difficulty to expertise** — scaffold novices (higher success rates), then fade and raise difficulty.
- **Prefer frequent low-stakes formative retrieval** over high-stakes exams.
