---

## Bottom Line

The front end and back end of learning rest on different evidence footings. The strongest-supported ideas here are Self-Determination Theory (autonomy/competence/relatedness), the *existence but high variability* of feedback effects, and formative assessment; the most over-sold are growth-mindset average effects and the Kirkpatrick causal chain from "smile sheets" to business results. Feedback is the connective tissue but a double-edged sword — Kluger & DeNisi's meta-analysis (d ≈ 0.41 average) found over 38% of feedback-intervention effects were *negative*. The hard, usually-unmet goal is transfer: ~94% of training evaluation stops at Level 1 (reaction), reaction correlates weakly with on-the-job behavior, and historically as little as ~10% of training transfers. The honest move is to design backward from what people must *do* and measure there.

---

## Core Principles

- **Design the front end for autonomy, competence, and relatedness** (SDT first) — the empirical anchor of the motivation literature.
- **Engineer feedback to feed forward, at the task/process/self-regulation levels — never the ego.** Over a third of feedback interventions backfire.
- **Constructively align objectives, activities, and assessment** (Biggs); make the assessment *be* the target performance.
- **Beware the intrinsic/extrinsic tension:** tangible, expected, contingent rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation; verbal/informational feedback enhances it.
- **Evaluate beyond the smile sheet, and treat transfer as the real goal** — manage the work environment, not just the event.

## Deep Dive

### Motivation Models

**Keller's ARCS** (formalized 1987, *JID*; *Motivational Design for Learning and Performance*, 2010) synthesizes motivation into four categories with strategies: **Attention** (perceptual/inquiry arousal, variability), **Relevance** (goal orientation, motive matching, familiarity), **Confidence** (learning requirements, success opportunities, personal control), **Satisfaction** (natural/positive consequences, equity). Extended to **ARCS-V** (adding **Volition**, 2008). Best treated as a well-organized design heuristic — originally field-tested in just two teacher-education programs; Keller himself called for "more controlled studies." Confidence cross-references Bandura's self-efficacy; Satisfaction cross-references SDT competence and feedback.

**Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan)** holds that three **basic psychological needs** — **autonomy, competence, relatedness** — are essential nutriments for autonomous motivation and well-being. The **organismic integration** continuum runs **amotivation → external → introjected → identified → integrated → intrinsic**. Among the most empirically supported motivation theories. The **undermining/overjustification effect**: Deci (1971) first showed rewards could reduce intrinsic motivation; Cameron & Pierce (1994) argued it was minimal; **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin*; 128 studies)** found engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28** respectively), as did all tangible and expected rewards, while **positive verbal feedback enhanced** free-choice behavior (**d = 0.33**) and interest (**d = 0.31**). Bounded conclusion: tangible, expected, contingent rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation; informational feedback supports it. This is the key link between motivation and assessment — over-rewarding and over-testing corrode the intrinsic motivation that sustains self-directed learning.

**Expectancy-Value Theory (Eccles & Wigfield):** motivation = **expectancy of success × subjective task value**, with value decomposed into **attainment, intrinsic, utility, and cost** (cost historically neglected). Renamed **Situated EVT** (2020). If expectancy or value is near zero, motivation collapses.

**Growth Mindset (Dweck) — the honest picture.** *Mindset* (2006) argues believing ability is malleable improves achievement/resilience. **Sisk et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*):** the mindset–achievement correlation was weak (~1% of variance); the intervention effect was weak, **d ≈ 0.08** — with more benefit for low-SES and at-risk students. **Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*; National Study of Learning Mindsets):** a pre-registered, nationally representative RCT found small average effects but meaningful *targeted* effects — improving grades among lower-achievers and advanced-course-taking among higher achievers — larger where peer norms supported the message. "Small but real and conditional," not transformative. Practitioner takeaway: target it to struggling learners and pair with genuine support.

**Flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990):** optimal experience arising when **challenge and skill are balanced** (both above average), with clear goals and immediate feedback — overlapping ARCS, SDT competence, and formative assessment. **Supporting ideas:** Malone & Lepper (1987, challenge/curiosity/control/fantasy); Hidi & Renninger's **four-phase interest model** (triggered situational → maintained situational → emerging individual → well-developed individual); Bandura's self-efficacy underpins ARCS Confidence and EVT expectancy.

### Feedback

**Hattie & Timperley (2007, *RER*):** feedback is among the most powerful influences on achievement but with wide variance. Effective feedback answers **"Where am I going?" (feed up), "How am I going?" (feed back), "Where to next?" (feed forward)** and operates at four levels — **task, process, self-regulation, self/personal**. The first three are productive (process and self-regulation especially); **self-level praise is least effective**. **Kluger & DeNisi (1996, *Psychological Bulletin*; 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations):** feedback improved performance on average (**d ≈ 0.41**) but **over 38% of effects were negative**. Their **Feedback Intervention Theory**: feedback that directs attention to the **self** (ego) rather than the **task** harms; it's also less effective for difficult/unfamiliar tasks.

**Formative assessment.** **Black & Wiliam (1998, *Assessment in Education*; "Inside the Black Box"):** formative assessment produces learning gains larger than most interventions (**effect sizes ≈ 0.4–0.7**), disproportionately helping low attainers. Wiliam's *Embedded Formative Assessment* (2011) gives classroom strategies. Caveat: grades alongside comments tend to wipe out the comments' benefit. **Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick (2006, *Studies in Higher Education*):** seven principles of good feedback that make learners self-regulating — the key shift being that students are *generators* of feedback, not passive recipients.

### Assessment

**Scriven (1967)** coined the **formative/summative** distinction (Bloom applied it to mastery learning). **Validity** (measures what it claims, supports the inference) and **reliability** (consistency) — in workplace assessment, reliability comes from *sampling* (many observations/assessors), not standardization. **Constructive alignment (Biggs 1996):** intended learning outcomes, teaching/learning activities, and assessment all target the same verbs, leveraging **"backwash"** (students study what they expect to be assessed) — the same logic as backward design (Phase 07). **Rubrics:** analytic (per-criterion, diagnostic, more reliable, slower) vs. holistic (single judgment, fast, less diagnostic); poorly written rubrics fragment complex performance and create false precision. **Authentic assessment (Wiggins):** tasks mirroring real-world practice, prizing transfer and judgment.

### Evaluation of Training / Did It Work?

**Kirkpatrick's Four Levels** (1959–60 ASTD series; *Evaluating Training Programs*, 1994): **Reaction → Learning → Behavior → Results**. The **New World Kirkpatrick Model** (2016) adds "required drivers" and starts from Level 4. **The critiques:**
- **Alliger & Janak (1989, *Personnel Psychology*):** the three baked-in assumptions (ascending information value; causal linkage; positive intercorrelation) are not supported.
- **Alliger et al. (1997, *Personnel Psychology*; 34 studies, 115 correlations):** correlations among levels are generally low; **"utility-type reaction measures were stronger correlates of transfer than were measures of immediate or retained learning,"** while affective reactions ("I enjoyed it") correlate weakly — the empirical death blow to satisfaction smile sheets as effectiveness proxies.
- It is a **taxonomy, not a causal model**, and practice skews shallow: per Rossett (2009), **94% of evaluation happens at Level 1, 34% at Level 2, 13% at Level 3, only 3% at Level 4.**

**Extensions/alternatives:** **Phillips ROI** adds **Level 5** (monetary return; ~5–10% of programs warrant it). **Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method** (2003) — purposive sampling of most/least successful cases. **Kaufman's five levels** add societal/mega impact. **Thalheimer's *Performance-Focused Smile Sheets* (2016):** redesign Level 1 with behaviorally anchored choices so it yields decision-useful data. **The transfer problem — Baldwin & Ford (1988, *Personnel Psychology*; ~70 studies):** transfer is shaped by **trainee characteristics, training design, and work environment**; their opening frame: of up to $100 billion spent annually on training, "not more than 10%... actually result in transfer to the job" (citing Georgenson 1982). Trainee characteristics and work environment act *directly* on transfer — the **post-training environment is decisive**, tying straight to Phase 07's "design backward from what people need to DO."

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Strongest:** SDT (autonomy/competence/relatedness; reward-undermining bounded); the *existence* of feedback effects; formative assessment (ES 0.4–0.7).
- **Contested/over-sold:** growth-mindset average effects (d ≈ 0.08; meaningful mainly for at-risk learners; Sisk–Yeager/Dweck dispute over practical significance unresolved); the Kirkpatrick causal chain.
- **Boundary-dependent:** rewards undermine mainly when tangible/expected/contingent; feedback harms in >38% of cases (self-focused, complex/unfamiliar tasks).
- **Evaluation models are largely taxonomies, not validated causal theories** (Kirkpatrick, Moore).
- **Measurement of transfer and of affective/spiritual outcomes is genuinely hard**; over-measurement distorts behavior (Goodhart's law) and can undermine intrinsic motivation.
- **Publication bias** likely inflates effects (flagged in the growth-mindset meta-analyses); weight pre-registered, large, replicated work.

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
*Motivating busy clinicians:* use SDT — protect autonomy (choice of topics/formats), build competence (case-based practice with feedback), leverage relatedness (peer learning); anchor to real patient problems (ARCS Relevance, utility value); beware mandatory-credit structures that crowd out intrinsic motivation. *Workplace-based assessment* climbs **Miller's pyramid** (knows → knows how → shows how → does): **mini-CEX** (Norcini et al. 1995/2003; ~15 min observation + 5 min feedback, seven competencies; **~10–14 encounters** and ~8 assessors for high-stakes reliability), **DOPS** (procedural skills; ~3 assessors), **multisource 360° feedback** (~8–12 raters; self-ratings notoriously uncorrelated with others'), **EPAs** (ten Cate 2005; 5-level supervision/entrustment scale), and **programmatic assessment** (van der Vleuten & Schuwirth 2005/2012; many low-stakes points aggregated). *Did practice change?* **Moore's outcomes framework** (2009, *JCEHP*) — 7 levels (participation, satisfaction, learning, competence, performance, patient health, community health) — distinguishes competence ("shows how") from performance ("does"). Cervero & Gaines (2015) find CME does improve performance and (less reliably) patient outcomes, rising with interactivity, multiple methods/exposures, and duration — and that education alone rarely changes practice without organizational reinforcement (a Baldwin & Ford transfer point).

### Ministry / Teaching
*Motivating volunteer/adult learners* (almost entirely intrinsic/identity-motivated): SDT and EVT dominate — protect autonomy (voluntary participation), build relatedness (belonging is often the primary draw), avoid extrinsic-reward structures; use attainment value (connection to identity and core values). *Formative feedback:* Nicol's principles and Hattie & Timperley's three questions apply directly; Csikszentmihalyi's flow conditions help design discussions neither boring nor overwhelming. *Assessing spiritual formation — be honest about limits:* much that matters is affective, long-horizon, and not reducible to scores; over-measuring risks Goodhart distortion and can undermine intrinsic motivation. Assess what you can (knowledge, observable practices, cautious self-report, longitudinal narrative/portfolio); evaluate a class or series with a modestly-adapted Moore/Kirkpatrick logic (performance-focused reaction à la Thalheimer; learning; behavior over months) while accepting the deepest outcomes are only partially measurable.

### Creative / Content
*Intrinsic motivation and the undermining effect are central* — creative work is the paradigm case where tangible, contingent rewards and heavy external evaluation crowd out the intrinsic motivation that fuels originality (Deci/Ryan; cf. Amabile). Protect autonomy and intrinsic/attainment value; use informational feedback (enhances), not controlling rewards (undermine). *Self-assessment and critique:* build feedback literacy (Nicol) — generate and use feedback, run structured critiques, self-assess against exemplars rather than chase praise; Hattie & Timperley's caution applies sharply (self/ego feedback like "you're so talented" is weak and induces risk-aversion; task/process feedback drives improvement). *Measuring engagement:* completion/dwell, return rate, shares, conversions, qualitative response — the analog of Kirkpatrick 1–4, with the same warning that shallow reaction metrics (likes ≈ smile sheets) correlate weakly with durable audience behavior.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **SDT first:** design for autonomy, competence, relatedness.
- **Feedback feeds forward, never to the ego** — three questions (where going / how going / where next); >38% of feedback backfires.
- **Constructively align** objectives, activities, and assessment; make the assessment the target performance.
- **Keep formative assessment low-stakes; minimize grade-everything regimes** that crowd out intrinsic motivation.
- **Evaluate beyond the smile sheet:** redesign Level 1 (Thalheimer), always measure Level 2, commit to Level 3 for programs that matter, reserve Level 4/5 for high-cost/high-visibility programs.
- **Treat transfer as the goal** — build supervisor/peer support, opportunity to perform, and reinforcement after the event.
- **In medicine, climb Miller's pyramid** with sampled workplace-based assessment (mini-CEX, DOPS, MSF, EPAs); evaluate against Moore's performance/patient levels.
