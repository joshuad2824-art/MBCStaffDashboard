---

## Bottom Line

Good presentation craft is Phase 05 and Phase 09 made concrete: the strongest slide method (Michael Alley's assertion-evidence model) is literally Mayer's multimedia principles plus signaling applied to slides, and good delivery is rhetoric's fifth canon (*actio*). The unifying rule is to *reduce extraneous load on the audience* (clean, single-message slides; no verbatim reading) while *the speaker carries the narrative*. The evidence base is wildly uneven: assertion-evidence/multimedia has real, replicated experimental support (Cohen's d up to ~1.5 in medical-education studies) but mostly in science/engineering/medical student populations; most "presentation-design philosophy" (Reynolds, Duarte, Atkinson) is expert craft wisdom, not trials; anxiety-reappraisal ("get excited") is solid; **power posing's hormonal/behavioral claims FAILED replication, and the "7%-38%-55%" Mehrabian rule is a genuine myth when applied to communication generally.** Principles beat rigid rules.

---

## Core Principles

- **Reduce the audience's extraneous load; let the speaker carry the narrative.** The single rule behind every robust recommendation.
- **Sentence headline + visual evidence, no bullet lists** (assertion-evidence) — the best-evidenced slide structure.
- **Don't read your slides** — the redundancy effect is the real "death by PowerPoint."
- **Slides are not documents** — build sparse speaker slides plus a separate leave-behind ("slideument" problem).
- **Principles over rigid rules** — 6×6, 10/20/30, the glance test are training wheels; mastery is one-idea-per-slide and audience-centeredness.
- **Body-language advice is myth-heavy** — treat Mehrabian's 7-38-55 and power posing with skepticism.

## Deep Dive

### The Case Against Bullet-Driven Slides

**Tufte** (*The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*, 2003/2006) argued the default templates impose a style that "usually weaken[s] verbal and spatial reasoning" — low information resolution, a rigid sales-pitch bullet hierarchy, "relentless sequentiality," and decorative "phluff." His cited case is the NASA *Columbia* slide; the *Columbia* Accident Investigation Board itself faulted "the endemic use of PowerPoint briefing slides instead of technical papers." **The cognitive mechanism is the redundancy effect** (Mayer, Heiser & Lonn 2001, *JEP* — narration plus duplicate on-screen text produced worse retention and transfer than narration alone). Boundary condition: **Mayer & Johnson (2008)** found *brief* 2–3-word labels next to a graphic can *help* (d ≈ 0.47–0.70) — the harm is from full verbatim text. **Doumont's rebuttal** ("Slides Are Not All Evil," 2005): Tufte conflates oral vs. written communication, the product vs. the tool, and his own evidence is weak — the problem is design, not software. Honest synthesis: bullet-dumps read aloud are genuinely harmful; the tool is neutral.

### The Assertion-Evidence Model

**Alley's** core principle: each content slide carries (1) a **sentence headline** stating the single main *assertion* (~8–14 words, ≥28pt) and (2) **visual evidence** (photos, diagrams, graphs, equations) — *not* bullets; speaking detail goes in the notes. The empirical backing — the one presentation method with genuine experimental support:
- **Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell & Muffo (2006, *Technical Communication*):** converting phrase headlines to sentence headlines produced significant retention gains (one item: 23% → 57% correct).
- **Garner & Alley (2013, *IJEE*; N=110, identical narration):** assertion-evidence beat common-practice slides on immediate comprehension (9.39 vs. 6.73, **d=0.81**), delayed recall (**d=0.89**), fewer misconceptions (**d=0.47**), and lower perceived cognitive load (**d=−0.50**) — though the text group did slightly better on *immediate statistical recall* (AE put statistics in the narration only).
- **Medical education (strongest effects):** Issa et al. (2011, *Medical Education*) found significantly greater retention gains for multimedia-principle slides; Issa et al. (2013) found large effects — immediate retention **d=1.49**, immediate transfer **d=0.76**, durable 4-week transfer **d=1.17**, retention **d=0.79**.

*Limits to state plainly:* samples are small-to-moderate and concentrated in science/engineering/medical *students*; Gabrielle's critique notes the AE comparison was confounded (control slides mixed text-only and text-plus-picture; AE slides had animations the controls lacked), and statistics were better recalled from on-screen text. These qualify but don't overturn the convergent finding. **Why it works:** assertion-evidence is Mayer's signaling + redundancy + multimedia + coherence principles operationalized — especially strong for technical/medical content where the evidence is naturally visual.

### Slide & Visual Design Philosophies (craft wisdom grounded in cognitive principles)

**Reynolds (*Presentation Zen*, 2008):** signal-to-noise ratio, *kanso* (simplicity through elimination), *ma*/empty space, picture superiority, "amplification through simplification," and "naked" presenting (sometimes a black screen) — plan analog, find the one core message, "the presentation should be worthless without you." **Duarte (*slide:ology*, 2008):** the **Glance Test** (grasp a slide in ~3 seconds), turning words into diagrams, the "big four" (contrast, repetition, alignment, proximity), whitespace as "the oxygen of a slide," one idea per slide. **Atkinson (*Beyond Bullet Points*, 2005):** a three-act story template plus sentence headlines with narration in notes — grounded in Mayer; best for persuasive/keynote contexts.

**Core slide-specific fundamentals:** one idea per slide; the glance test; high signal-to-noise; clear visual hierarchy and contrast; high-resolution images (picture superiority); progressive builds (cross-ref segmenting, Phase 05); consistency. **Rules vs. principles:** the 6×6 rule and **Kawasaki's 10/20/30** (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30pt font — originally for VC pitches) are useful training wheels that force editing and large type, but arbitrary if reified. Better to ask "how many slides do I *need*?" **Data viz for the stage:** one message per chart, strip chartjunk, highlight the relevant comparison, label directly, never project a dense table. **The "slideument" problem:** slides are not documents — build sparse speaker slides plus a separate leave-behind (Duarte's "slidedoc," Tufte's technical report), resolving the "but I need the detail for later" objection.

### Delivery Mechanics

Delivery is rhetoric's fifth canon (*pronuntiatio/actio*). **Voice:** vocal variety, the **power of the pause** (the best cure for fillers), volume, articulation. **Fillers** ("um/uh"): the problem is excess; the fix (Seals lab, 2022, *Advances in Physiology Education*) is record-and-analyze, more preparation, deliberate silent pauses replacing fillers. **Body — and the myths:**
- **The Mehrabian "7-38-55" myth.** The claim that communication is "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language" misapplies Mehrabian's 1967 studies, which concerned *only* the resolution of *incongruent single-word emotional cues* (judging liking/feeling), with tiny all-female samples. Mehrabian himself said the equations don't apply "unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes." Applying "93% nonverbal" to presentations is simply false.
- **Power posing — failed replication.** Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010, *Psychological Science*; N=42) claimed expansive poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and increased risk tolerance. Ranehill et al. (2015, N=200) found the *felt-power* effect but **no hormonal or behavioral effects**; Simmons & Simonsohn (2017) found no evidential value for behavioral/physiological effects; first author **Dana Carney publicly disavowed** it (2016: "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real"). Only the subjective "felt power" effect survives. A textbook replication-crisis case.

**Managing anxiety.** Glossophobia is near-universal (Chapman Survey of American Fears: 25.3% "afraid or very afraid"; ~5–10% severe). Evidence-based: **anxiety reappraisal** — Brooks (2014, *JEP:General*): saying "I am excited" (vs. "calm" or nothing) improved felt excitement, opportunity mindset, and *performance* (anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal — easier to reframe than extinguish). **Arousal reappraisal** (Jamieson et al.): teaching that stress arousal is functional improves challenge responses and performance; a 2024 meta-analysis (*Scientific Reports*) found a small significant benefit (d ≈ 0.23). **Preparation, rehearsal, and exposure** remain first-line.

**Rehearsal:** deliberate practice (rehearse aloud to time; record yourself for fillers/pace; seek feedback). **Extemporaneous** delivery (rehearsed but not memorized, from sparse notes) generally beats memorization (robotic, blank-risk) or verbatim reading (kills connection). **Q&A:** repeat/reframe the question for the room, answer concisely, bridge to the key message; for hostile questions stay calm and non-defensive; build interaction every ~10 minutes (participation drops after ~30 minutes of monologue).

### Physical and Contextual Delivery

Know the room (sightlines, back-row legibility — the practical root of the 30pt heuristic, lighting, a clicker, standing front-and-center). Sometimes the best option is **no slides** (or a black screen — the "takeaway"). **Virtual:** look at the lens (strategically), camera at eye level with front lighting, push energy up ~30%, shorten and chunk (~half in-person length; interaction every ~10–15 min), and design slides for a small screen (question/answer-led, one idea per slide).

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Real, replicated:** assertion-evidence/multimedia (d up to ~1.5 in medical-education studies) — but limited to small, mostly technical/medical student samples, with at least one confounded comparison.
- **Craft wisdom, not trials:** Reynolds, Duarte, Atkinson — persuasive and theory-aligned but largely unproven; cited business "results" (pitch-deck conversion rates) come from vendors and should be discounted.
- **Solid:** anxiety/arousal reappraisal (Brooks 2014; Jamieson; 2024 meta-analysis d ≈ 0.23).
- **Myth-heavy "body language":** Mehrabian 7-38-55 (misapplied) and power posing (behavioral/hormonal claims failed replication; only "felt power" survives).
- **Cultural variation:** delivery norms aren't universal — eye contact, gesture, directness, personal space, and acceptable expressiveness vary; Western coaching advice is culturally loaded.
- **Style over substance** and **accessibility** (minimalism must keep sufficient contrast, legible type, no meaning-by-color-alone, captioning — cross-ref Phase 13).

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
Assertion-evidence is the strongest fit — the evidence base is literally medical-student data (Issa et al.), and clinical "evidence" is inherently visual (scans, Kaplan-Meier curves). Headline the clinical takeaway ("Adjuvant therapy halved recurrence at 2 years"), show the figure, put the full table in the handout. For grand rounds, in-services, tumor boards, and conference talks, lead with the visual. For EHR/workflow training, show the screen and the action and narrate the steps — never paste dense procedure text.

### Ministry / Teaching
Lean on delivery (*actio*) and narrative; use slides sparingly or not at all in worship/teaching where presence and the spoken/read word carry the moment (a black screen or no slides is often better). Where slides help (adult Bible study, a lecture), one image/verse per slide — never bullet-dumped scripture you then read aloud (redundancy). Voice (pace, pause, variety) and open, natural body presence matter more than any deck.

### Creative / Content
Reynolds/Duarte image-driven decks; Atkinson's three-act storyboard for persuasive pitches. On camera (podcast, video, webinar), apply the virtual mechanics — lens eye contact, eye-level framing, +30% energy, chunked interaction. Show the work (portfolio images, demos) rather than describing it.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Sentence headline + one visual per slide;** detail goes in notes and a separate leave-behind.
- **If you can't write a one-sentence headline for a slide, it has no single point** — cut or split it.
- **Never read your slides;** brief labels next to a graphic are fine, full verbatim text is not.
- **Rehearse aloud to time; record once for fillers/pace; get one round of feedback.**
- **Pre-talk, reappraise** ("I'm excited"; "this energy is helping me") rather than trying to calm down.
- **Make no promises about power posing or "93% nonverbal"** — both are myths/failed replications.
- **Override Western delivery norms with local ones for cross-cultural audiences.**
