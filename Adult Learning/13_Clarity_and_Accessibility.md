## Bottom Line

Clarity and accessibility are the "reach-everyone" floor of communication: plain language, health literacy practice, Universal Design for Learning (UDL), and accessibility standards (WCAG) share one premise — assume your audience may struggle, and design so the information can be found, understood, and used the first time. The evidence is strongest for plain language and teach-back (good, consistent), well-established for the health-literacy/outcomes link, weakest/most-misused for readability formulas, and conceptually strong but thinly proven for UDL. The single biggest pitfall is treating a readability grade-level score as proof of comprehension — formulas measure only surface features and can be gamed. And designing for the margins improves the experience for everyone (the "curb-cut effect"): captions, plain language, large print, and alt text help non-native speakers, older adults, distracted multitaskers, and search engines, not just people with disabilities. Build accessibility in from the start; retrofitting is costlier and worse.

---

## Core Principles

- **Plain language is "find, understand, and use the first time"** — now an international standard (ISO 24495-1:2023) and U.S. law (Plain Writing Act 2010).
- **A readability score is a screen, never proof of comprehension** — formulas measure surface features only and can be gamed.
- **Shift from "screen the patient" to "universal precautions"** — assume everyone may struggle; confirm with teach-back.
- **Design for the margins (the curb-cut effect)** — accessibility helps everyone and carries SEO/reach dividends.
- **Clarity is an ethical floor** — obscurity excludes and, in health, harms; but beware oversimplification that loses necessary nuance.

## Deep Dive

### Plain Language

Plain language "puts readers first" so they can find, understand, and use information the first time — now codified in **ISO 24495-1:2023** (four principles: relevant, findable, understandable, usable; explicitly subordinating "mechanical measures such as readability formulas"). In the U.S., the **Plain Writing Act of 2010** (PL 111-274) requires federal agencies to write "clear, concise, well-organized" communication; OMB M-11-15 directs them to the Federal Plain Language Guidelines and plainlanguage.gov. **Evidence is good and consistent:** Kimble's *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please* compiles ~50 studies showing plain language saves money and that readers "understand it better and faster, are more likely to comply, and more likely to read it." Core techniques: short sentences, common words, active voice, direct "you," most-important-first, informative headings, lists, defined jargon. The root cause of expert obscurity is the **curse of knowledge** (cross-ref Phase 09).

### Readability Formulas and Their Proper Use

Major formulas: **Flesch Reading Ease** (1948; ASL + syllables/word; 0–100, higher = easier); **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level** (1975, U.S. Navy; built into Word); **SMOG** (1969; polysyllable count; uniquely calibrated for ~100% comprehension, the de facto health standard, best with ≥30 sentences); **Gunning Fog** (1952); **Dale-Chall** (familiar-word list); **ARI**; **Lexile**. **Targets:** AMA recommends **6th grade** for health materials (Weiss 2003); NIH cited as 6th–8th; CDC ~8th. Per AHRQ, "the average adult reads at the 8th or 9th grade level, and 20% read at the 5th grade level or below." Yet materials fall short: Rooney et al. (2021, *Health Promotion Practice*; 2,585 journal patient-education materials) found mean readability grade 11.2–13.8 — only **8.2% met the NIH 8th-grade target and 2.1% met the AMA 6th-grade target.**

**The critical caveat — the most important point here.** Formulas measure only **surface features** (word/sentence length or word familiarity); they do NOT measure comprehension, coherence, organization, design, or prior knowledge. **Redish** (2000) notes they were developed on children's schoolbooks and ignore reader differences, content, and layout. **Schriver:** people game the score without improving comprehension; writing to lower a grade score produces **choppy, disconnected text that can be harder to understand** (Duffy-Kabance, Charrow studies — scores dropped several grades with no comprehension gain). Even AHRQ and ISO subordinate formulas to reader testing. **A grade-level score is a rough screening signal, never proof of clarity.**

### Health Literacy (Major Focus)

**Healthy People 2030** split the construct into **personal** health literacy (the ability to find, understand, and use information for health decisions) and **organizational** health literacy (how equitably organizations enable this) — emphasizing *use*, *equity*, and *organizational* responsibility. **Scope:** the **2003 NAAL** (~19,000 adults) found only **12% proficient** and **~36% at basic/below-basic health literacy (~77 million adults)**, with stark disparities (28% of White vs. 65% of Hispanic adults at basic/below). **Outcomes:** Berkman et al. (2011, *Annals of Internal Medicine*; AHRQ Evidence Report 199) found low health literacy consistently associated with more hospitalizations and ER use, fewer preventive services, poorer adherence, and higher mortality among seniors — a **well-established** link (~80 million U.S. adults have limited health literacy).

**Frameworks and tools:** the **AHRQ Universal Precautions Toolkit** (assume everyone may struggle); **teach-back** (Tool 5 — ask patients to restate in their own words; Ha Dinh et al. 2016, *JBI* — positive effects on knowledge, adherence, self-efficacy, inhaler technique, "not always statistically significant"; **good and improving evidence, the leading practice**); **Ask Me 3**; the **CDC Clear Communication Index** (20 scored items, 90+ passes); **PEMAT** (Shoemaker, Wolf & Brach 2014 — scores understandability and actionability, ≥70% acceptable; α=0.71; actionable materials gave higher comprehension 76% vs. 63%); and assessment instruments (REALM, TOFHLA, Newest Vital Sign). **Designing for low health literacy:** plain language + visuals/pictographs + teach-back; chunk; lead with what the patient must *do*; apply numeracy/risk best practices (Phase 12 — natural frequencies, icon arrays).

### Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

UDL extends **universal design** (coined by architect **Ron Mace**; the curb cut as the classic example) to learning. **CAST** (Rose, Meyer) built it on three principles tied to brain networks: **Engagement** (the why), **Representation** (the what), **Action & Expression** (the how). **Guidelines 3.0** (July 2024) reframed the goal from "expert learners" to **learner agency**, added **identity** to learner variability, and addressed bias — stressing it's "not a prescription or checklist." **Evidence — be honest:** widely adopted and conceptually strong, but rigorous experimental evidence is limited and mixed. **Capp's 2017 meta-analysis** (18 studies) found UDL improves the learning *process* but "the impact on educational outcomes has not been demonstrated." **Edyburn (2010):** "an analysis of research evidence establishing UDL as a scientifically validated intervention is not possible." **Verdict: strong framework, weak causal evidence — a design heuristic, not a proven intervention.**

### Accessibility (Digital and Document)

**WCAG** (W3C WAI) is the international standard, built on four **POUR** principles — **Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust** — with conformance levels **A / AA / AAA**. WCAG 2.0 (2008), **2.1 (2018)**, 2.2 (Oct 2023) are additive; **2.1 AA is the most-referenced legal benchmark**. **Legal context:** Section 508 (federal ICT, keyed to WCAG 2.0 AA); the ADA (interpreted to cover websites — DOJ's 2024 Title II rule requires WCAG 2.1 AA for state/local government, compliance 2026–2027); the European Accessibility Act (effective June 2025). **Practical accessibility:** alt text; contrast (4.5:1 normal, 3:1 large — Phase 11) and never color alone; keyboard navigability and visible focus; captions/transcripts; proper heading hierarchy and semantic markup; accessible PDFs/Word docs (heading styles, alt text, reading order, tagged PDFs); accessible tables. **Scope and the curb-cut effect:** ~1 in 4 U.S. adults (~26%, ~61M) has a disability, rising to ~2 in 5 over 65. The 2016 Oregon State / 3Play study (n=2,124) found **75% of students use captions as a learning aid** — for focus, retention, and comprehension, ranking far ahead of hearing loss. Accessibility carries real **SEO and reach benefits** (semantic HTML, alt text, transcripts are indexable).

## Evidence Base & Open Debates

- **Strong/consistent:** plain language (Kimble) and teach-back; the health-literacy/outcomes link (Berkman et al. 2011).
- **Weak/most-misused:** readability formulas — valid only as surface screens, routinely mistaken for comprehension proof; gaming them often makes text worse.
- **Conceptually strong, thinly proven:** UDL (Capp 2017 — process yes, outcomes not demonstrated).
- **The simplicity-vs-accuracy tension is real and under-appreciated:** Devaraj, Marshall & Wallace (ACL 2022) — "more readable but inaccurate versions... may be worse than no access"; Shardlow & Nawaz (ACL 2019) — automated simplification "omitted 30% of critical information" in clinical texts. Most acute in medicine, law, theology.
- **Standards are consensus/legal, not experimental** — but the underlying need (millions of disabled and low-literacy users) is well-documented; compliance can become box-ticking (test with real AT users).
- **Reading level is only one dimension** — culture, numeracy, trust, motivation, and tech access also shape usability.

## Application by Context

### Clinical / Training
The major application. Apply AHRQ Universal Precautions across discharge instructions, consent forms, medication labels, and tip sheets; use **teach-back**; assess materials with **PEMAT**; combine text with pictographs; communicate risk with natural frequencies and icon arrays (Phase 12); ensure patient portals/EHR materials meet WCAG AA and offer large-print/translated versions. Target ~6th-grade reading level (AMA), but treat the formula score as a screen only and test with real patients.

### Ministry / Teaching
Use UDL as a *design heuristic* (multiple means of engagement/representation/expression) while recognizing its thin outcome evidence; provide large-print bulletins, captioned/recorded sermons, high-contrast slides with minimal text, and plain-language teaching; design for diverse congregations including older adults and people with disabilities; balance simplicity with theological accuracy (the simplicity-vs-accuracy tension applies directly).

### Creative / Content
Add alt text, captions, and transcripts to blogs, podcasts, and video; write clearly for a broad audience; use semantic structure; reap the SEO/reach dividend. The curb-cut effect means accessibility work doubles as reach work.

## Quick-Reference Heuristics

- **Write to plain-language defaults:** short sentences, common words, active voice, "you," most-important-first, headings, lists, defined jargon.
- **Run a readability formula (SMOG for health) as a screen only,** then evaluate with **PEMAT** or the **CDC Clear Communication Index** (target 90+).
- **Test with 3–5 actual representative readers** — the single highest-value step; if comprehension/task completion is below ~80%, revise regardless of a good score.
- **Always confirm verbal instructions with teach-back.**
- **Target WCAG 2.1 AA;** bake in alt text, captions/transcripts, contrast ≥4.5:1, keyboard operability, and heading structure at creation time.
- **Preserve essential caveats while cutting clutter** — guard against oversimplification, especially in medicine and theology.
