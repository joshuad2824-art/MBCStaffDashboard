# Phase 02 — Lettering, Custom Type & Wordmarks: The Craft of Drawing Letterforms

## TL;DR
- **Lettering, calligraphy, and type design are three distinct disciplines**: calligraphy *writes* letters (single gesture, the tool does the work), lettering *draws/builds* letters as a custom image for one use, and type design makes a *repeatable system* where every letter must work with every other. A logotype is lettering, not type design — and per Jessica Hische, "lettering is essentially illustrations of letters, words, and phrases."
- **The craft is dominated by optical correction and spacing-by-eye** — well-grounded craft consensus rooted in real, measurable perceptual illusions. Round/pointed letters overshoot ~1–3% (Phinney: an O exceeds an H "by about 1–2%," an A apex "about 3% taller"; Karow recommends 3% for O, 5% for A); horizontals are drawn thinner than verticals (Phinney: verticals made thicker "on the order of 10%"); junctions are notched; crossbars/waists sit above mathematical center. "We read with our eyes, not with rulers, so the eye should win every time" (Tobias Frere-Jones).
- **For most small-business clients, full custom lettering is NOT the responsible default**: it costs more and takes longer. The smart middle path — licensing a strong typeface and customizing key letterforms — yields an ownable, distinctive wordmark at a fraction of the cost, and is what most situations actually call for.

## Key Findings

1. **The three disciplines are separated by *method*, not tools.** Calligraphy is writing (each stroke a single gesture, no revision); lettering is drawing (built up with as many strokes, corrections, and erasures as needed); type design is building a system. The defining test for lettering: rearrange the letters and it would look terrible, because the art is made for that one configuration only.

2. **Letters are built skeleton-first, then "flesh."** The standard construction sequence (Hische's *In Progress*: "The Skeleton / The Body / The Clothes") starts from a monoline armature, then adds weight/stroke, then details (serifs, terminals).

3. **Optical correction is where amateurs fail, and it is real perceptual science, not lore.** A peer-reviewed 2019 paper (de Waard, Van der Burg & Olivers, *Vision*, "A Thickness Illusion: Horizontal Is Perceived as Thicker than Vertical") confirmed that people overestimate horizontal-line thickness — the exact illusion type designers compensate for.

4. **Spacing a logotype is "the rhythm of black and white," done by eye.** The governing principle (James Edmondson / OH no Type Co.): "Counterspace Equals Letterspace" — the space *between* letters should visually match the space *inside* them, judged by the evenness of the rhythm, not by metrics. A few words get hand-spaced far more carefully than running text ever does.

5. **You should never just set a font and call it a logo** — legally and professionally. In the US, typeface *designs* aren't copyrightable (only the font *software* is), but font licenses (EULAs) and platform rules (e.g., 99designs) require customization, and customization is what makes the mark distinctive, trademark-able, and ownable.

6. **The digitization workflow is deliberate, not auto-traced**: thumbnails → refined pencil/Procreate sketch → scan/import as template → redraw with the pen tool placing anchor points on the extrema (never Image Trace) → refine curves, weight, spacing.

7. **Named masters give you a concrete curriculum** (see Details): Jessica Hische, Doyald Young, Martina Flor, Ken Barber/House Industries, Tobias Frere-Jones, Gerard Huerta.

## Details

### 1. The Three Disciplines — Lettering vs. Calligraphy vs. Type Design

**Calligraphy = writing letters.** As Letterform Archive puts it: "calligraphy is written, and lettering is constructed by carving, stitching, painting, drawing, or any other method of hand-making. With calligraphy, each stroke is produced by a single gesture." The tool (broad-edge or pointed nib, brush) and the gesture *do the work* in real time; there is no revising. Thick/thin contrast emerges from pressure (pointed pen) or pen angle (broad nib).

**Lettering = drawing letters.** Joseph Alessio (Smashing Magazine): lettering is "the art of drawing letters… a specific combination of letterforms crafted for a single use and purpose as opposed to using previously designed letters as components, as with typography." You sketch, erase, correct, and build up each letter with as many strokes as needed. Critically, lettering is closer to **illustration** than to typography. Jessica Hische's definitive framing (from her FAQ): "Lettering is essentially illustrations of letters, words, and phrases. As a letterer, when I'm hired to draw the word 'holiday' I don't first draw the entire alphabet… then position the letters… I draw the word as a unique image. This means that in a lot of lettering, if you rearrange the letters it would look pretty terrible — the art is meant to be seen and used in that specific configuration and that configuration only."

**Type design = building a repeatable system.** Hische: "Typeface designers work very differently. They have to create a system of letters that can be endlessly rearranged and work together… Type designers have to make typefaces that even the least design-savvy person can work with and set beautifully." Gerrit Noordzij's famous definition of typography is "writing with prefabricated characters"; Peter Bil'ak notes this "implies a complete distinction from lettering, handwriting or graffiti… which don't offer a repeatable system."

**Why a logotype is lettering, not type design.** A wordmark is a few fixed letters in one fixed arrangement. Gerard Huerta states the principle exactly: "When you design a font, you have to design a letter to be its lowest common denominator because it has to work with every other letter. When you do a logo, each of those letters are in a fixed position and can really kind of be the best they need to be." This is the single most important conceptual distinction in this phase: a logotype's letters can be *optimized for each other* in a way a font's letters never can.

**The "few words vs. a system" / economics distinction (Hische).** Hische's classic example: if you love a one-weight display typeface for a book cover, setting the title, subtitle, and author name in it directly "would just not work" — fine details blow out at small sizes and look "horsey" when huge. "Bring in a letterer to make you exactly what you need… You can manipulate type to really fit the exact mold for your project." Lettering revived precisely "because of people's need to customize."

**How the three inform each other.** Calligraphic foundations underlie both. The contrast model of most letters (where the thicks and thins fall, the stress/axis) is inherited from the broad-nib pen (angled stress, e.g. old-style serifs) and the pointed pen (vertical stress, thicks on downstrokes). Even drawn, non-calligraphic letters carry this DNA. Many letterers recommend learning pointed-pen calligraphy first because it builds the eye for *why* beautiful letters look the way they do, which you then translate into *drawing* them.

### 2. The Mechanics of Drawing Letters

**Skeleton → body → clothes.** Hische works "a mono-weight 'skeleton' at the center of the letterforms (I work this way for scripts, always starting with a single line), and for other lettering work I start by drawing basic shapes like circles and rectangles and combining and making adjustments from there." Her book's structure literally names the stages: **The Skeleton** (the armature/centerline), **The Body** (adding weight/stroke to the skeleton), **The Clothes** (serifs, terminals, decoration).

**Stroke logic and contrast.** Where weight goes and how thick/thin alternate is the inheritance of the writing tools (above). Monoline = no contrast; high-contrast = dramatic thick/thin (e.g., a Didone or a Vogue-style mark). The *stress/axis* (the angle of the thin parts of an O) signals the model: angled = broad nib/old-style, vertical = pointed pen/modern.

**Terminals, joins, branching, bowls, spurs, serifs.** These are the "clothes" and the structural connections. Key construction-quality markers: where a bowl branches off a stem (b, p, R), how arches spring (n, m, h), the spur on a G, the serif bracket shape. Consistency comes from **repeating shapes** — the same bowl used on b/d/p/q, the same arch on n/m, the same terminal on a/c/f — so the set looks like a family. Doyald Young loved drawing the "lowercase, two-weight roman letter 'g'" as the hardest test of these relationships.

### 3. Optical Correction (the critical section)

The master principle, from Tobias Frere-Jones (*Typeface Mechanics 001*): "Our conscious minds want to draw one shape, but our eyes need to see another. Part of typeface design is managing this eternal friction between logic and optics… we read with our eyes, not with rulers, so the eye should win every time."

**Overshoot.** Round letters (O, C, S, e, o) and pointed letters (A, V, W) must extend slightly above the cap-height/x-height and below the baseline to *look* the same size as flat letters (H, E, x). Frere-Jones explains the mechanism: "only a narrow sliver of an O is the full height, and the rest of the shape falls away. The parts that are too short greatly outnumber the parts that are big enough, so we conclude — wrongly, but very reliably — that the round shape is too small."
- **Typical amounts**: Thomas Phinney (*Communication Arts*, "Know If a Font Sucks"): "the O generally exceeds the cap height and baseline of an H by about 1–2%… Pointy elements like the sharp apex of an A only look like they are the right height when they are about 3% taller." Peter Karow's *Digital Formats for Typefaces* recommends 3% for O and 5% for A (per Wikipedia's "Overshoot (typography)"). The commonly cited general range is ~1–3% of cap/x-height for round letters.
- **Overshoot scales with curvature**: flatter/"slower" curves need less, sharper/pointier ones need more (Frere-Jones; TypeDrawers practitioners).

**Horizontal strokes drawn thinner than verticals.** Our eyes overestimate horizontal thickness, so to *look* equal, horizontals must be drawn thinner. Phinney (*Communication Arts*): to make monoline letters look truly even, "the type designer needs to make the vertical strokes a bit thicker, on the order of 10%." Fábio Martins (Scannerlicker) cites a subtler ~1% in some monoline cases. The exact figure varies by design and weight — judge by eye. This is confirmed perceptual science (de Waard et al., *Vision*, 2019).

**Diagonals are heavier than orthogonals.** A 45° diagonal of the same width looks heavier than a vertical and thinner than a horizontal; the correct width is roughly the in-between value. Scannerlicker's "quick and dirty" construction: draw an ellipse with the vertical stroke's width and the horizontal stroke's height, then make the diagonals tangent to it.

**Junctions / "trapping."** Where strokes cross or meet at acute angles (the apex of A, the join of V/W/Y, the center of M, the crossing of X), mass piles up and reads as a dark spot. The fix is to thin/notch the strokes near the join — the optical cousin of the printing **ink trap** (a notch cut into an interior corner so ink doesn't fill it; Matthew Carter's Bell Centennial, 1978, is the canonical example). Toshi Omagari: "Letters like M and W have lots of joints which can look visibly heavier because of the mass there. You can reduce the black spots by spacing out the strokes… or cut deep into the joints." Even when not literally notched, the connecting strokes into a V or join are tapered slightly.

**Off-center middles (optical centering).** The optical center of a vertical space is *above* the mathematical center; anything placed at true center looks too low. So the crossbar of an H sits slightly high; the waist/junctions of B, E, R, P, K sit above center; the crossbar of A sits lower than H's (to avoid top-heaviness); the upper bowl of B, S, and the figure 8 is drawn *smaller* than the lower one. Helvetica's H crossbar is set above center and its S lower curve is larger — rotate them 180° and the asymmetry becomes obvious (Typographica; H&Co). This same "gravity effect" is why a wordmark inside a circular logo is nudged above center.

**Curves vs. straights.** Curved strokes look thinner than straight ones of equal width (same reason curves need overshoot — they enclose more white and carry less mass), so curves get a slight weight increase, smaller than the horizontal/vertical correction.

**Evidence grade.** Optical correction is **craft consensus grounded in strong perceptual evidence**. The horizontal-thickness illusion and the round-looks-smaller illusion are experimentally demonstrated; the *specific amounts* (1%, 3%, 10%) are craft rules-of-thumb that vary by design — which is why every master ends on the same note: the eye, not the ruler, is the final judge. Doyald Young: "I don't care what the rules are. And there are lots of rules. The ultimate rule is how does it look? Does that 'O' look bigger than the 'N'?… And you have to get them to keep on drawing letters until they see the difference."

### 4. Building and Customizing a Wordmark/Logotype

**Modify a font vs. draw from scratch.** Drawing fully custom yields a maximally distinctive, ownable, scalable mark but costs the most time/money. Modifying an existing typeface is faster and cheaper and is often sufficient. The legal/practical backdrop: in the US, typeface *designs* are not copyrightable (US Copyright Office Circular 33: the Office "cannot register a claim to copyright in typeface or mere variations of typographic ornamentation or lettering"), and only the *font software* is protected. But the EULA can still forbid logo use, and platforms like 99designs *require* original lettering: "If you create a wordmark without customizing the typeface, it will be considered copyright infringement." Practically: a wordmark that is just a font, untouched, is neither distinctive nor defensible — so you customize.

**Why custom/customized is worth it (when it is).** Custom lettering can be optimized per-letter for the exact name, tuned for size/media (favicon to billboard), incorporate brand motifs, and — because it's original artwork — is far easier to trademark and defend than a generic font setting. Doyald Young's blunt version: "If you use a font, a client's competitor has the same opportunity. You haven't created a unique image."

**Techniques for customizing**: redraw or swap key letters; build custom ligatures and connections; adjust weight, width, and proportion; add a distinctive detail (a single cut, a wave, a unique terminal). Gerard Huerta's Pepsi work is the textbook case — he "was hired by Pepsi to take the four letters they had and flesh them out into an entire lower case font," and the custom "e" echoes the Pepsi wave.

**Spacing a logotype — the rhythm of black and white.** You space the **counters and gaps to an even rhythm**, by eye, not by metrics. James Edmondson (OH no Type Co.): the "Counterspace Equals Letterspace" rule — the gap between two letters should visually equal the counter (white) inside them — and bolder letters need *tighter* spacing. His "Three at a Time" method: judge spacing by looking at three letters at once so each gap balances its neighbors. This is why a few words get vastly more spacing care than running text: in a logotype every gap is bespoke. (This deepens, rather than repeats, the kerning covered in the typesetting phase: here you may also *move the outlines themselves*, not just the sidebearings.) Young's related rule: letters in a word are "spaced from the top," because readers read the tops of words.

**Picking a good base typeface to customize.** Choose one whose skeleton and proportions already fit the brand (so customization is refinement, not a fight), that has a distinctive-but-legible character, and ideally avoid the most ubiquitous defaults. The base should give you strong bones; you supply the uniqueness through spacing, proportion, and a few redrawn details.

**Workflow from word to finished logotype** (synthesizing Hische, Young, Flor):
1. Research/brief — what the name must feel like, where it will live, smallest size.
2. Thumbnails — many tiny, fast explorations of style and structure (Young produced "40 to 100 roughs in a few days," each in "less than a minute").
3. Choose a direction; refine a tight pencil (or Procreate) sketch at the most-used size (Young roughed at ~1.5 inches).
4. Solve structure, proportion, and spacing on paper/screen by eye.
5. Scan/import as a template layer.
6. Redraw in vector with the pen tool (points on extrema; see §6).
7. Refine curves, balance weight, apply all optical corrections, finalize spacing.
8. Present final art (Hische: "For logo projects, I always present final art to the client, never sketches").

### 5. Script, Brush, and Monoline Lettering

**Script and connected letterforms.** Script lettering is "any type of letterform that connects to another by an exit and an entry stroke" (Envato/Tuts+). The logic of connections (the ductus, entry/exit strokes) and consistent slant are what make it read as a flowing whole. **Flourishes** are built from ovals and added at natural exit/entry points — ascenders, descenders, terminal strokes, the dot of an i — and should connect logically and never collide (Lettering Daily; Ken Barber teaches flourished script at Type@Cooper using 18th–21st-century models). "Counterspace Equals Letterspace" applies beautifully to script — the rhythm of the connections enforces even spacing.

**Brush lettering / brush script vs. pointed-pen calligraphy.** Brush *calligraphy* is written with a flexible brush/brush-pen (thick on downstrokes via pressure, thin on upstrokes), giving the energetic, painterly, modern brush-script look. It differs from pointed-pen calligraphy mainly in tool and feel: the brush is bulkier, more gestural and casual; the pointed pen is finer and more formal (Copperplate/Spencerian). Brush *lettering* (drawn, then filled/refined) differs again — it's constructed, not written. The classic energetic brush-script logo (think mid-century soft-drink and sign-painting scripts) descends from the sign-painter/show-card tradition.

**Monoline lettering.** Uniform stroke weight, no contrast. Clean, friendly, modern; common in contemporary wordmarks and signatures. Monoline is the hardest test of optical correction precisely because there's nowhere to hide: horizontals must be thinned, junctions notched, and curves slightly thickened, or the "even" weight will look uneven.

**Grids, guides, and construction lines.** Letterers work over baselines, cap-height, x-height, ascender/descender lines, and slant guides. But guides are a starting frame, not a cage. A useful nuance from the Ecker/OH no practitioners: set guides *after* a first pass at plotting points, because "when you set guidelines before setting your points, you can get too mechanical and your lettering will feel stiff." The eye overrules the grid.

### 6. The Digitization Workflow (sketch to vector)

**The standard pipeline**: thumbnails → refined pencil/Procreate sketch → ink or clean pencil → scan/photograph → place as a template layer → **redraw** with the pen tool → refine. **Never auto-trace** (Illustrator's Image Trace) — it produces far too many points and mushy curves.

**Bezier best practices for lettering:**
- **Points on the extrema** — the topmost, bottommost, leftmost, rightmost points of each curve (the N, S, E, W of an O). The "box method": draw a rectangle around each letter; wherever it touches is where a point goes.
- **Minimal anchor points.** Ryan Hamrick's maxim, quoted by Ecker: "Mo Beziers, mo problems." Fewer points = smoother curves and easier editing. (OH no Type Co.: "the purpose of well-built vectors is to maintain efficient editability.")
- **Handles horizontal/vertical at the extrema** so you can lock to 90° and edit cleanly; keep handles balanced (no single handle doing all the work).
- Jessica Hische's three vector tips (her Skillshare lesson) center on point placement and balanced handles; she works "mostly just the pen tool," no fancy tricks.

**Refining in vector**: correct curve tension, balance weight, apply optical corrections, finalize spacing. Pro tips from practitioners: print it out and view at multiple sizes; squint/blur your eyes to find "hotspots" attracting too much/little attention; check the path with the direct-selection tool to confirm smooth, minimal points.

**Tools**: pencil + tracing paper (Hische, Young, Flor all start analog or treat the iPad like paper); Procreate on iPad (Hische uses the "6B pencil" brush); Adobe Illustrator for vector lettering and logos; Glyphs or FontLab if it becomes a full font (Young finished in FontLab; serious type tools like RoboFont/Glyphs have "add extreme points" commands Illustrator lacks).

### 7. When Custom Is Worth It vs. Licensing a Typeface (the business judgment)

**The cost/benefit.** Custom lettering is the most expensive and time-consuming route; it buys a distinctive, ownable, scalable, defensible mark. Licensing/lightly customizing a typeface is faster and cheaper and is the *responsible* default for most small businesses. Real-world logo budgets: DIY/AI makers ~$0–50; a solo/freelance designer commonly "a few hundred dollars… upwards of $2,500" (Fiverr); design studios should "budget a minimum of $2,500" (Fiverr); high-end agencies "likely between $50,000 to $100,000" (Fiverr). Research cited by Fiverr: "the majority (65%) of businesses would only pay up to $500 for a new logo, with just 15% willing to pay over $1,000 for their design." A bespoke hand-lettered wordmark sits at the upper end of freelance pricing and well into agency territory.

**When a wordmark genuinely needs custom lettering:** when the brand lives or dies on distinctiveness (fashion, spirits, consumer packaged goods on a shelf), when it must be trademark-defensible, when it needs bespoke size/media variants, or when a specific personality can't be found in any existing face. When it does *not*: tight budget, short-lived or unproven venture, a name that reads fine in a well-chosen face, or a client who needs many fast iterations first.

**The middle path (recommended default).** License a strong typeface and customize it for the logotype — redraw one or two key letters, tune the spacing by hand, adjust weight/width, add one distinctive detail. This is exactly how a great deal of professional wordmark work is actually done; it delivers ownability and personality without the full custom cost.

**Advising a friend or client.** Stage it: (1) Start with the name set in 2–3 well-chosen faces to find the right *voice* and proportions. (2) If one feels right, customize it — bespoke spacing plus a few redrawn details — and confirm the font license permits logo use (or pick one that does / is bespoke-friendly). (3) Reserve full from-scratch lettering for when distinctiveness, longevity, and legal defensibility clearly justify the cost. Always design the wordmark to work in **one color on any background** first; color and symbols come later. The benchmark that should change the recommendation toward full custom: the mark will carry significant brand equity (retail/packaging/national presence) and must be legally defended — at that point the custom investment pays for itself.

### Named Masters — the specific works to study

- **Jessica Hische** — *In Progress: See Inside a Lettering Artist's Sketchbook and Process, from Pencil to Vector* (2015; revised ed. 2022). Study the skeleton/body/clothes construction, her vector point-placement tips, and her lettering-vs-type FAQ. Drop-cap work: the "Daily Drop Cap" project. Logos/film: *Moonrise Kingdom* titles, Tilda typeface.
- **Doyald Young** — *Logotypes & Letterforms* (1993), *Fonts & Logos* (1999), *Dangerous Curves: Mastering Logotype Design* (2008). The master of hand-drawn logotype construction and curves. Study Prudential (his own favorite — he widened it in "5%… 10%… 10 units… 15 units" steps, weighted between Century Book and Century Bold), the GE corporate font (with Don Bartels), and the Lynda/LinkedIn "Creative Inspirations: Doyald Young, Logotype Designer." His method: soft-pencil roughs at ~1.5″, tight pencil tissue, then digital; legibility first, then uniqueness; "the ultimate rule is how does it look?" His precision: on Prudential he kept "a stack of 1500 sheets… each page might have a dozen changes," moving points "1/1,000th inch" on a "1,000 by 1,000 square." His purpose, from *Dangerous Curves*: "The only way to create a logotype that is truly unique is for the designer to transcend the limitations of the available fonts and typefaces on the market." Marian Bantjes (AIGA, 2009) on his hand: "perfect letterforms drawn in pencil at a surprisingly small size without so much as a mark of hesitation or awkwardness."
- **Martina Flor** — *The Golden Secrets of Lettering: Letter Design from First Sketch to Final Artwork* (2017). Best for the full process and the *business* of lettering; chapters run from "the DNA of letters" through sketching, vectorizing, and going pro. Co-founder of "Lettering vs Calligraphy." MA in type design, Royal Academy of Art, The Hague.
- **Ken Barber / House Industries** — *House Industries Lettering Manual* (2020). House's chief lettering officer and type-design director; the manual covers history, anatomy, serif/sans/brush/script methods, case studies of real logos, and getting paid. Teaches at Cooper Union; runs flourished-script workshops at Type@Cooper.
- **Tobias Frere-Jones** — the *Typeface Mechanics* essays at frerejones.com (001 on overshoot/alignment; 002 on weight). The clearest writing on optical compensation. Typefaces to study: Gotham, Whitney, Interstate, Tungsten, Retina.
- **Gerard Huerta** — custom logotypes and mastheads: AC/DC, *Time*, *People*, HBO, Pepsi font, Swiss Army. Study his logo-vs-font distinction and the album-lettering/CBS Records tradition.
- **Tradition to know**: the sign-painting / show-card lettering lineage (the root of brush-script and casual lettering); Letterform Archive (San Francisco) as a study collection; Type@Cooper and TypeEd for courses; Doyald Young's mentors Mort Leach and the lineage back to mid-century lettering schools.

## Recommendations

1. **Build the eye before the hand.** Spend your first effort learning to *see* optical corrections: take Helvetica's H and S, rotate them 180°, and watch the asymmetry appear. Do the overshoot test (circle vs. square at equal height). This single skill separates competent lettering from amateur work.
2. **Learn the skeleton → body → clothes sequence** from Hische's *In Progress*, and practice constructing the same word in 3–4 styles to internalize that lettering is image-making, not typesetting.
3. **Drill the vector discipline now**: points on extrema, minimal points, balanced horizontal/vertical handles, never auto-trace. Hische's vector tips and the OH no Type "Drawing Vectors" guide are the fastest path.
4. **For your own brand and for friends/small businesses, default to the middle path**: set the name in 2–3 strong faces, pick the best voice, then customize (spacing by hand + one or two redrawn letters + a distinctive detail). Only escalate to full custom lettering when distinctiveness/longevity/legal defensibility clearly justify it.
5. **Always confirm the font license** before building a wordmark on a licensed face; when in doubt, customize enough to make it unmistakably original, and design in one color on any background first.
6. **Reference library to acquire, in order**: Hische *In Progress*; Flor *The Golden Secrets of Lettering*; Barber *House Industries Lettering Manual*; Young *Dangerous Curves* (or *Fonts & Logos*); plus the free Frere-Jones *Typeface Mechanics* essays online.
7. **Benchmarks that change the call**: budget under ~$500 and an unproven venture → well-chosen/lightly-customized face; brand equity riding on a shelf/retail presence + need for trademark defense → invest in full custom lettering.

## Caveats

- **Evidence grading**: The core of this phase is **craft consensus** — the tested professional judgment of master letterers refined over centuries. This is appropriate and reliable here. Crucially, it is **grounded in strong perceptual evidence**: the horizontal-thickness illusion (de Waard, Van der Burg & Olivers, *Vision*, 2019) and the round-looks-smaller illusion are experimentally demonstrated. The eye genuinely misjudges mathematically equal shapes.
- **The numbers are guidelines, not laws.** Overshoot "1–3%," verticals made "~10%" thicker than horizontals, A-apex "3–5%" — these are starting rules-of-thumb that vary by typeface style, weight, width, and size. Every master converges on the same caution: trust the eye over the math. Treat any single percentage as a sanity-check, not a target. (One figure I encountered secondhand — Verdana's verticals at "198 vs. 176 units," an 89% ratio — could not be confirmed against a primary source; Microsoft's own documentation describes only a "slightly modulated stroke weight," so treat that specific number as unverified.)
- **Myth/overstatement to avoid**: framing optical corrections as rigid mathematical formulas (e.g., a fixed "golden" overshoot for all designs) is overstated — Frere-Jones and Young both explicitly reject ruler-driven design. Also avoid the common myth that "you can't use any font in a logo because fonts are copyrighted" — in the US the *design* isn't copyrighted; the real constraints are the license (EULA), trademark, and the practical/competitive need to be distinctive.
- **US-centric law**: the "typeface designs aren't copyrightable" point is specifically US law. Germany (since 1981), the UK, and others *do* protect typeface designs; advise international clients accordingly.
- **Some sourcing is secondhand**: a few specific craft details (e.g., the "break curves into segments" technique attributed to Young's *Dangerous Curves*) come from reader summaries rather than the primary text and should be verified against the books themselves. The strongest primary sources here are Frere-Jones's own essays, Hische's own writing/FAQ, Young's recorded interviews and book statements, and the peer-reviewed perception paper.
- **AI/automation note**: this report describes the human craft; auto-trace and AI logo makers can produce serviceable results for low-stakes needs but do not replicate the per-letter optimization and optical correction that define professional custom lettering.
