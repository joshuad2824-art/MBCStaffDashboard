# Phase 04 — The Logo Design Process: From Blank Page to Delivered Mark

## TL;DR
- **There is a real, repeatable seven-stage workflow** — Discovery → Research → Ideation (sketch) → Convergence/Construction → Color & Suite → Testing → Presentation & Delivery — and the single most important rule, agreed on by nearly every master identity designer, is **do not open the computer until you have done discovery and sketched on paper.** The process, not raw talent, is what separates professional results from amateur ones.
- **Most of this knowledge is "craft consensus"** — the well-tested judgment of master designers (Rand, Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv, Bierut, Airey, Draplin, Glitschka) — rather than empirical science. The honest exceptions: golden-ratio/Fibonacci "construction" overlays are largely **myth/marketing theater** when retrofitted, and the famous **one-option-vs-many-options presentation question is genuinely contested**.
- **The process scales:** a friend/small-business job can run a compressed 1–2 week version; a full professional identity engagement runs ~4–12 weeks (logo) or 4–6 months (full brand). What must never be cut, even in the fast version, is discovery and testing.

---

## Key Findings

1. **The workflow is well-established and converges across sources.** David Airey's *Logo Design Love* structures it as groundwork → pencil-to-PDF → presentation; Glitschka's *Vector Basic Training* as "thumbnail → reference → sketch → refine → vector"; Sagi Haviv's firm as brief → problem definition → exploration judged against three criteria. They differ in emphasis but agree on the spine.

2. **Skip-the-computer-and-sketch is the most universal piece of advice in the field** — and it has modest empirical support, not just folklore.

3. **Discovery is the highest-leverage stage and the most commonly skipped.** A strong creative brief functions as a "contract of intent." Comprehensive briefs are associated with materially fewer revision rounds.

4. **Construction grids are legitimate engineering when used to build and refine a mark; they are theater when retrofitted to justify it.** The Yahoo 2013 "blueprint" reveal is the canonical cautionary tale. The golden ratio is the most overstated claim in logo construction.

5. **Design in black and white first, then add color** — near-universal craft consensus, because color masks structural weakness.

6. **The deliverable is a *suite/system*, not a single logo** — primary lockup, secondary/alternate, standalone mark/icon, and favicon, each in color, one-color, and reversed versions, plus clearspace and minimum-size rules.

7. **The one-option vs. multiple-options debate is real and unresolved.** Paul Rand and (substantially) Sagi Haviv and Michael Bierut favor conviction in one direction; many working designers and studios present 2–3 routes with a recommended one. Both camps agree on one thing: **never present "options soup" with no rationale.**

## Details

### Stage 1 — Discovery & The Creative Brief

**The goal of discovery is to find the real problem, not to take dictation.** Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv frame the entire intake around defining what they call "the design problem" — "we work in the service of clients and address the challenges and parameters they bring to us." David Airey's first chapter is built around understanding the client before drawing anything; he warns that jumping straight into design "can lead to misunderstandings and missed goals."

**The intake questionnaire.** Across professional templates (Airey, JUST Creative, The Logo Creative, htmlburger, Really Good Designs), a strong logo questionnaire covers:
- **Business basics:** "Describe your business/service in one sentence" (designers repeatedly call this the hardest and most vital question); years in business, history, the name's origin and meaning.
- **Why now:** for a redesign, *why* change? (Airey devotes a chapter to "skirting the hazards of a redesign," citing the Tropicana rebrand as a cautionary failure.)
- **Audience:** who are the customers; who do they *want* to attract.
- **Competitors:** who are they, and does the client want to follow the category or break from it.
- **Personality/attributes:** the most useful technique is the **semantic-differential scale** — pairs of opposing adjectives (e.g., minimalist↔ornate, playful↔serious, modern↔heritage) with a neutral midpoint, where the client marks where the brand sits. This converts vague taste into a usable direction.
- **Where the logo will live:** signage, app icon, embroidery, vehicle, packaging — this dictates constraints (e.g., a multi-color logo gets expensive on merchandise).
- **Practical scope:** taglines, additional deliverables, deadline, budget, decision-maker.

**The brief as a "contract of intent."** Airey's deeper point (developed in *Identity Designed: The Process*, 2024, which organizes identity work into four stages — research, strategy, design, implementation) is that the written brief keeps designs *focused* and becomes the reference both sides return to. Crucially, Airey warns: "don't allow your client to confuse this as a chance to dictate terms; instead it's an opportunity to really focus on the project."

**Handling difficult clients.** Identify the **actual decision-maker** early (Airey notes that with larger orgs your contact is often not the decision-maker, and you'll later be introduced to a committee — a known risk). For the client who "knows what they want," the questionnaire and semantic scales redirect from prescribed solutions to underlying goals. Marty Neumeier's reframing helps: in *The Brand Gap* (2003) he writes, "A brand is not a logo. A brand is not a corporate identity system. It's a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or company." So discovery is about the gut feeling you're trying to create, not the client's pixel preferences.

> *Cross-reference: the principles being served here (appropriate, simple, memorable, etc.) and the 7-type taxonomy are covered in Phase 03.*

### Stage 2 — Research & Analysis

**Competitive/visual audit.** The professional move (well-described by branding agency DeSantis Breindel) is to gather competitors' logos, colors, typography and imagery from public sources, then isolate each element onto comparison boards — all the logos together, all the type together, a color-frequency chart. **Patterns reveal "ownable whitespace."** Their OneSpan example: competitors clustered on bright blue, leaving a purple/violet zone open. The audit serves two purposes: find differentiation space, and avoid accidental resemblance.

**Moodboards define the visual territory** *before* designing. Best practice (Ave, Studio1, Nielsen Norman Group) treats moodboards as **working documents, not presentations**, and ideally develops **multiple distinct boards representing genuinely different directions** — then asks the client which feels *right for their audience and ambition*, not which they personally "like." This reframing from preference to strategic fit is the key technique. Note one genuine disagreement among pros: some (e.g., The Creative Accent) advise *not* looking at competitors during moodboarding to avoid derivative work, on the theory that strategy already told you where to stand.

**Positioning.** Neumeier's "zag" ("when everybody zigs, zag") is the strategic frame: the mark should stake out a position the category has left open.

### Stage 3 — The Divergent / Ideation Phase

**Why sketch before vector — the case, stated plainly.** Airey: "By removing the computer from the creative process, you gain much more freedom… Translating the same process to a computer requires unnecessary steps that hinder your creative flow." Glitschka built an entire book (*Vector Basic Training*) around the thesis that relying on software for creative work produces "toolers" rather than designers: "drawing out your ideas, analyzing the shapes, and then methodically building them precisely in vector form." Jacob Cass: "you can't design a logo by just hopping straight onto the computer… brainstorm and sketch first."

There is **modest empirical support**: design-education research (Appiah & Cronjé, 2012, *Art, Design & Communication in Higher Education*) found manual thumbnailing *before* design aids idea development, and lamented that students increasingly sketch *after* designing on computer (or not at all) — backwards from the intended method.

**Ideation techniques, concretely:**
- **Mind-mapping / word association:** Airey describes writing a central brief word and branching outward to form "as large a 'thought cloud' as possible" — the raw material for sketching. Glitschka teaches the same (word lists, lateral thinking).
- **Lo-fi thumbnails / quantity drills:** small, fast, "down and dirty" sketches. The discipline is **quantity over polish** — "draw the obvious to get past it." Common guidance lands around **20–50 thumbnails per idea**; Glitschka and others recount logos taking **"at least a hundred hand sketches."** One documented case (bioTrekker) explored **60+ solutions** before narrowing to a handful.
- **Draplin's approach:** rapid, prolific sketching in (of course) Field Notes notebooks, moving fluidly between iterations; his Skillshare "family crest" class walks the process from research → geometric structure → type → color.
- **Bierut's notebook method** (see below) is the most rigorously documented version of "think with your hands."

**Divergence then convergence.** Generate many directions, *then* narrow. Airey's rule for the narrowing: **don't show the client your throwaways** — "there will undoubtedly be directions you don't want to pursue, and it would be most unfortunate if the client chose one of those."

**Bierut's documented practice.** Michael Bierut has kept a single continuous, numbered series of standard composition notebooks since **August 12, 1982**. In his own words (*Design Observer*, "26 Years, 85 Notebooks," 2008): "On August 12, 1982, I took a 10 x 7 1/8 inch National Blank Book Company composition book from the supply closet of my then employer, Vignelli Associates. From that moment, I have never been without one." He was on **Notebook #85 by 2008**, and reportedly had filled **108 notebooks by 2015** (AIGA *Eye on Design*, on his book *How To*). He insists they are "notebooks," not sketchbooks — mostly meeting notes, phone numbers, specs, with sketches interspersed — and uses them strictly in chronological order, "the projects all jumbled together." His honest reflection on process: "Looking back at the books, I'm surprised at how often I got the idea for certain solutions at the very beginning… When I'm feeling smug and self-satisfied, I tell myself that, like Mozart, my ideas emerge fully formed… In more common, less secure, moments, I wonder if I'm lazy, if I settle too soon." For the Hillary Clinton "H" he deliberately chased radical simplicity (*Design Observer*, "I'm With Her," 2017): "I wanted something that you didn't need a software tutorial to create, something as simple as a peace sign or a smiley face. I wanted a logo that a five-year-old could make with construction paper and kindergarten scissors."

### Stage 4 — Convergence & Construction

**From rough to tight.** The chosen thumbnail(s) get developed into refined sketches, then digitized. Glitschka's vector method is the most systematic: a "refined sketch" becomes the roadmap; the vector is then built with deliberate anchor-point discipline (his "Prime Point Placement" and "Clockwork Method," and a "Vector No-Fly List" of bad point habits).

**Construction grids — the honest treatment.** A construction grid is **geometric documentation that makes design decisions visible and reproducible** — it shows how shapes relate, documents curve radii and spacing, and helps at handoff to sign-makers, embroiderers, and developers (GridMe). Used *during* building, this is genuine rigor.

**But grids used *after the fact* to justify a finished mark are decoration — "design theater."** The canonical example is **Yahoo's 2013 rebrand**, whose behind-the-scenes "mathematical blueprint" video showed geometry that "looked retrofitted" and was widely mocked; the logo was criticized regardless. The honest distinction: *"A grid used during the process to build the mark is engineering. A grid drawn after the mark is finished to justify it is decoration. The two look almost identical in a presentation."*

**The golden ratio is the most overstated claim in the field.** Multiple debunkings (Fast Company's coverage of David Cole's Apple-logo analysis; designers' own teardowns) show the golden ratio is **retrofitted onto successful designs far more often than used to create them** — Apple's logo was *not* built on it, and "you can get circles to fit into anything." Honest verdict (graded **MYTH/OVERSTATED**): the golden ratio reliably produces *a* harmonious proportion, but it is neither a secret formula for beauty nor evidence of rigor. VistaPrint's guidance is sound: "Don't use construction grids to make a weak design look stronger to a client" and "don't apply mathematical ratios to a logo where they don't exist."

**Optical correction is the real rigor.** Because the eye doesn't see like a ruler, designers deliberately *break* mathematical perfection: curved letters/shapes (O, C, S, circles) **overshoot** the cap height or they look too small; the crossbar of a perfectly circular "G" gets nudged; the PlayStation "P/S" overlap is redrawn to look right rather than be geometrically aligned. *"The best logos succeed because they look right, even when they aren't strictly 'right.' Optical balance always trumps rigid geometry."*

### Stage 5 — Color & The Logo Variations / Suite

**Black and white first.** Strong craft consensus: design the mark in one color (black on white) first, then add color. The reason is diagnostic — color can "compensate for weak structure," so a mark that works in pure black is structurally sound. Airey reinforces designing distinctive marks in black and white; presentation PDFs should "help the client focus on the idea, and not on an easily changed aspect like color."

**The suite/lockup system — the actual deliverable checklist:**
- **Primary logo/lockup** — the most complete version, used most often.
- **Secondary/alternate lockup** — usually the *opposite orientation* of the primary (if primary is horizontal, secondary is stacked/vertical, and vice versa) for tight spaces.
- **Standalone mark/icon (brandmark/submark)** — the symbol alone, for small spaces and social avatars.
- **Wordmark** — name-only treatment, for when simplicity is required.
- **Favicon** — most minimal version, legible at 16×16 px.
- **Color treatments for each:** full-color, one-color/monochrome, and reversed/knockout (white) for dark backgrounds.
- **Clearspace** (exclusion zone) and **minimum sizes** defined. A common clearspace heuristic: empty margin equal to roughly half the height of the mark (and brands typically peg the unit to a letter height like the cap-height or first initial).

A practical "suite" is **2–5 variations working as one cohesive system** — same fonts, weights, line treatment across all.

**Color choice (kept brief by design).** At this stage, color is chosen for differentiation (per the competitive audit) and appropriateness to personality. Keep it practical here.

> *Cross-reference: deep color theory and full brand color systems belong to later phases; this phase covers only the logo-suite deliverable.*

### Stage 6 — Testing & Validation

The professional testing battery before finalizing:
- **Black-and-white / grayscale test** — does it survive without color?
- **Scalability test** — legible from **favicon (16×16 px)** to billboard; a common rule of thumb is recognizability at ~32×32 px and at business-card width (~1 inch). If detail disappears below ~100px width, simplify.
- **Reversal/knockout test** — works in white on dark, and on photographic/textured backgrounds.
- **Reproduction/print test** — print small and large on real materials; check embroidery/single-color stamp behavior; confirm no white-halo artifacts on transparent versions.
- **Squint test** — blur your eyes (or view at ~50% opacity / reduced size). A clear, confident silhouette should persist; if it "dissolves into visual noise," there's too much fine detail. This doubles as a negative-space check.
- **"Does it work in context" mockup test** — place the mark in realistic applications (signage, app icon, business card, web header, merch). Airey's case studies repeatedly show designers building Photoshop mockups; mockups also make concepts tangible for clients.

**Self-critique and distance.** Get away from the work and return with fresh eyes (Cass explicitly builds in "reflection" / maturing time; Haviv's firm emphasizes letting ideas develop). Neumeier offers two field tests: the **Swap Test** (swap your mark's element with a competitor's — if it's no worse, your mark isn't distinctive enough) and the **Hand Test** (cover the logo on a piece of communication — can you still tell who made it?).

> *Cross-reference: the black-and-white/silhouette/scalability/"describe over the phone" tests are introduced in Phase 03; this phase places them in the workflow.*

### Stage 7 — Presentation & Rationale (process-level)

**Where it fits:** after you've chosen, refined, tested, and mocked up — *then* you present. The core move is to **present the mark with a story/rationale, in realistic context**, not as a bare JPEG.

**The one-option vs. many-options debate (genuinely contested):**

*The one-option camp.* **Paul Rand** is the patron saint: in 1986, Steve Jobs paid Rand **$100,000** for the NeXT identity, and Rand delivered a single solution — a black cube tilted at 28° — inside a ~100-page rationale booklet. Per paulrand.design, Jobs recalled Rand telling him: "No, I will solve your problem for you and you will pay me. You don't have to use the solution. If you want options go talk to other people." Jobs's reaction is the argument for the method: reading the booklet, "I knew we had the solution… Rand gave us a jewel." The logic: presenting one option signals the designer's confidence, prevents the client from choosing a weak direction, and forces the designer to take real responsibility for a solution rather than outsourcing the decision. **Sagi Haviv** opens presentations with a warning slide; in his words (to Happy Ali): "We remind our clients — and we open every presentation with a slide that says — it's never love at first sight… A good logo, a good trademark, gains meaning and power over time." (His Chase Bank anecdote: the chairman initially hated the now-iconic mark, then later wore it on his cufflinks and cap.) **Bierut** substantially works this way too (parameters and conviction; Saks built entirely from one "adaptive reuse" idea; the Clinton "H" held to one direction despite internal pushback).

*The many-options camp.* Many working designers and studios present **2–3 distinct routes, each with rationale and usually a recommended one** (e.g., the studio Believe in: "we usually show up to three different design routes… each route supported by a clear rationale… often includes a preferred solution"). The argument: clients are human and "need choices" to feel buy-in; it can de-risk the relationship when trust isn't yet established. The famous counter-example is **Uber 2016**, where a designer reportedly presented ~200 options and the CEO picked one impulsively — the cautionary tale for *over*-optioning.

*Where the evidence lands (CRAFT CONSENSUS, contested):* The deciding variable is **trust and the designer's ability to defend the work.** Jacob Cass's evolution is instructive: early career he promised "five logos"; now he says "I'm going to give you the solution… I can suggest [fewer] and have a story behind why these work." The synthesis nearly everyone agrees on: **the number of options matters less than the rationale; never present undifferentiated "options soup" with no story.** Note the contested claim graded **MYTH/OVERSTATED:** that any *single* fixed number (one, or three, or five) is universally correct.

### Stage 8 — Delivery & Files (process-level)

What gets handed off at the end of the process:
- **The full logo suite** (all lockups, mark, wordmark, favicon) in **color, one-color, and reversed**.
- **File formats:** vector masters (AI/EPS/SVG/PDF) plus raster (PNG/JPG) for everyday use; **logotype converted to outlines** so the type renders without the font installed. (SVG for web, EPS/AI for print/production, PNG for transparency; never a JPG master.)
- **A logo package / brand sheet / mini style guide** documenting clearspace, minimum sizes, color values (HEX/RGB/CMYK/Pantone), correct/incorrect usage, and the variation set. Designers (e.g., Nela Dunato) stress that **shipping without usage guidelines leads to the work being "butchered"** in application.

### Stage 9 — The Process as a Whole

**Named end-to-end models (compared):**
- **David Airey:** groundwork/brief → research → pencil sketches → digitize to PDF → present → refine → deliver; *Identity Designed: The Process* frames the macro view as research → strategy → design → implementation.
- **Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv:** brief → define "the design problem" → explore concepts judged continuously against **appropriate / simple / memorable** (Haviv: "Is it appropriate? Is it simple? Is it memorable?"; note they treat *timelessness as a result, not a directive*) → present with rationale, warning "it's never love at first sight."
- **Von Glitschka:** define scope/brief → creative prep (word lists, mind-mapping) → thumbnails → refined sketch → systematic vector build → art-direct/refine → present → deliver.
- **Jacob Cass (JUST Creative):** strategy → research → sketch/ideate (sketch + experiment) → digitize → present with story → revise → deliver; ~2–3 weeks for an average logo project, 50% deposit / 50% on completion.
- **Paul Rand:** research → single solution → rationale booklet → present one option.

**Realistic timelines (and how the process scales):**
- **Friend / small-business quick job:** a compressed 1–2 weeks. Documented reality (Nela Dunato) — a friend's logo done in a week with a first-proposal acceptance is possible *when you know the person and the brief is tight*, but it's rare and not repeatable at scale.
- **Standard professional logo:** commonly cited as **2–6 weeks** (discovery → research → concepts → refinement → delivery), often 4–6 weeks; Cass cites 2–3 weeks.
- **Logo + brand system:** **4–12 weeks.**
- **Full brand identity from scratch / rebrand:** **4–6 months** (some complex rebrands 12+ months); a rebrand typically consumes **20–40 hours of the client's time**.
- The biggest schedule variable is almost always **client responsiveness and number of decision-makers**, not design time.

**Common process mistakes (the field's consensus failure modes):**
1. **Skipping discovery** — "designing blindfolded."
2. **Going to the computer too early** — premature polish, software bias, fewer ideas explored.
3. **Presenting too many options with no rationale** — "options soup"; abdicating the designer's judgment.
4. **No rationale / story** — presenting a bare image and letting the client react on personal taste.
5. **Skipping testing/mockups** — discovering scalability or reversal failures after launch.
6. **Retrofitting golden-ratio/grid overlays** to fake rigor.
7. **Shipping files without usage guidelines.**

## Recommendations

**For the practitioner (you), staged and concrete:**

1. **Build two reusable assets now:** (a) a one-page **discovery questionnaire** containing the business-in-one-sentence question, the "why now," audience, competitors, a **semantic-differential adjective scale**, where-it-will-live, and scope/deadline/decision-maker; and (b) a **deliverables checklist** (the suite + file formats + mini style guide). These two documents are 80% of what makes you look professional.

2. **Adopt a fixed personal pipeline** and run *every* job through it, scaling depth to budget: Discovery → quick competitive audit + 1–3 moodboards → **paper thumbnails (aim for dozens; target ~50, accept "ugly")** → pick 1–3, refine → digitize one direction tightly → black-and-white first → build the suite → run the testing battery → present in mockups with a written rationale → deliver the package.

3. **On the options question, default to *one strong direction with a documented rationale* once you can defend your work** — it produces better outcomes and trains your judgment. Until your confidence/credibility is established (e.g., early friend-and-family jobs), it is legitimate to show **2–3 distinct routes with a clearly recommended one.** Never show more than three, and never show throwaways.

4. **Treat construction grids as build-tools, not sales props.** Use guides and geometry to *construct and optically correct* the mark; document real geometry for handoff. **Do not** add golden-ratio/Fibonacci overlays to impress — informed clients and peers see through it, and it will date your work.

5. **Always test before you present**, even on a free job: black-and-white, favicon-size, reversed, squint, and at least one real mockup. This single habit prevents the most embarrassing post-launch failures.

**Benchmarks that should change your approach:**
- If a client can't answer "describe the business in one sentence" or name competitors → **stop and do more discovery** before any sketching.
- If your mark dies at the squint test or favicon size → **return to construction**, don't proceed to color.
- If you find yourself generating options to avoid committing → that's a signal to **commit to one and write the rationale** instead.
- If the client has a committee/multiple decision-makers → **budget more time**, lock the brief in writing, and identify the true decision-maker before presenting.

## Caveats

- **Evidence grade for this phase is mostly CRAFT CONSENSUS**, which is appropriate — process knowledge is the accumulated judgment of master practitioners, not lab science. Treat the named designers' models as *well-tested expert heuristics*, not proven laws.
- **The strongest empirical claims are modest:** thumbnailing-before-design has supportive design-education research (Appiah & Cronjé), and comprehensive briefs are associated with fewer revisions — but these are not large, rigorous bodies of evidence.
- **Genuinely contested:** the one-vs-many-options presentation question (presented above from both sides). Don't let anyone tell you it's settled.
- **Graded MYTH/OVERSTATED:** golden-ratio/Fibonacci construction as proof of rigor; the idea that any single fixed number of thumbnails or options is universally correct; rigid "discovery is always N meetings" claims.
- **Survivorship bias warning:** the Rand/Haviv/Bierut "present one option" stories are told by, and about, designers with enormous reputational leverage and elite clients. A beginner doesn't yet command "you'll pay me and you don't have to use it" authority; calibrate accordingly.
- **Source-quality note:** much online "logo process" content is SEO marketing from logo-mill and AI-logo sites; the durable principles here are anchored to the named books and designers (Airey, Glitschka, Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv, Rand, Bierut, Neumeier, Cass), with web sources used mainly for corroboration.
- **Cross-references:** Phases 01 (typography), 02 (lettering/optical correction/sketch-to-vector), 03 (principles, taxonomy, tests) are covered; Phases 08 (presentation/critique/feedback/defense) and 10 (tools/files/production/handoff) carry the deep dives that this phase only addresses at the process level.
