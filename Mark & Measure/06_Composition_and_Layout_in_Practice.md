# Composition & Layout in Practice — Phase 06

## TL;DR
- **Master the grid as a tool for freedom, not a cage**: learn the six working grid types (manuscript, column, modular, hierarchical, baseline, compound), choose one by analyzing the content, and break it only deliberately for emphasis — the through-line uniting Müller-Brockmann, Vignelli, Samara, and Lupton.
- **Most composition craft (balance, visual flow, scale drama, white space, type/image integration) is well-tested craft consensus, NOT proven law** — while a smaller core (Gestalt grouping, size/contrast salience, the isolation effect) has genuine perceptual-science backing, and the reading-pattern models are mostly overstated except the F-pattern.
- **Format dictates craft**: a poster is one idea at a glance; an editorial spread is pacing across a system; a carousel is a first-slide hook plus swipe-continuity (the first slide carries roughly 80% of the weight); packaging is hierarchy on 3D panels under a point-of-purchase glance — and POPAI's 2012 Shopper Engagement Study found an all-time-high 76% of buying decisions are made in-store.

## Key Findings

1. **The grid is a problem-solving framework, not decoration.** There are six practical grid types; you choose by analyzing what content you must organize, not by aesthetic preference. The compound grid combines structures for complex publications.
2. **You must know the grid to break it well.** Deliberate grid-breaking (scale shifts, overlap, deliberate misalignment, working on an axis) creates emphasis and energy — but only against an established order the reader can sense.
3. **Visual hierarchy via size, contrast, position, and isolation is the most reliable lever you have**, and it is partly grounded in perceptual science (bottom-up salience and the isolation effect).
4. **Balance is a choice, not a default**: symmetrical balance reads as formal/stable/static; asymmetrical balance (managing unequal visual weights around a fulcrum) reads as dynamic/modern and is harder to execute.
5. **White space is an active design element.** Macro and micro white space, active vs. passive, are deliberate tools for focus, elegance, and reduced cognitive load — not wasted space.
6. **Text over image is a legibility-engineering problem** solved with scrims, overlays, blur, knockouts, heavy weights, and placement in calm image areas; aim for WCAG contrast (4.5:1 body, 3:1 large).
7. **The reading-pattern models deserve honest grading.** The F-pattern has real NN/g eye-tracking evidence but only for unformatted text-heavy reading (and it is a failure mode to design against); the Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram are craft heuristics with little empirical support and stop applying the moment you introduce real hierarchy.

## Details

### 1. Advanced Grid Systems in Practice

**The six working grid types and when to use each:**

- **Manuscript (single-column / block) grid** — one large text block plus margins; secondary structure positions folios, footnotes, running heads. Best for continuous reading: books, long essays, long-form articles. Because there's little structure to hide behind, typography (typeface, leading, measure, hierarchy) carries the design.
- **Column grid** — multiple vertical columns separated by gutters; best for *discontinuous* information (text, asides, pull quotes, images) that needs to coexist. The number of columns is a flexibility decision: more columns = more flexibility. On screen, the 12-column grid dominates because 12 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, and 6.
- **Modular grid** — a column grid with horizontal flowlines added, creating a matrix of modules. Best when a column grid can't control complexity: data-heavy layouts, catalogs, calendars, image galleries, e-commerce, corporate reports. Modules can be combined into larger zones.
- **Hierarchical grid** — irregular, content-driven structure with varying column/module sizes, often used when none of the above fit (portfolio sites, media-rich homepages, magazine feature openers). Hardest to construct well by eye; takes years of practice.
- **Baseline grid** — horizontal lines at consistent vertical intervals to which text sits, ensuring cross-column vertical rhythm. Used in combination with column/modular grids. (This applies Phase 01's leading discipline to multi-element page layout: set the baseline increment to the body leading, then key spacing, image edges, and captions to multiples of it for vertical rhythm.)
- **Compound / combination grid** — superimposing or combining grids (e.g., a column grid for running text plus a modular grid for data) for complex publications. Samara's *Making and Breaking the Grid* covers column, modular, and compound grids explicitly.

**Anatomy and construction terms**: columns, gutters (the gap — thinner gutters create more "visual pressure," wider gutters create calmer layouts), margins (the border space outside the live area; small margins create tension with the page edge — Vignelli prized this), flowlines (horizontal lines that start/stop content and create hanging points), spatial zones (groups of modules forming a compositional region), and markers (consistent positions for repeating elements: folios, running heads).

**Construction steps for an artifact:**
1. Inventory the content and its relationships (continuous vs. discontinuous; how many information types).
2. Choose the grid type from that inventory (long text → manuscript/column; mixed → column/modular; complex data → modular; irregular → hierarchical).
3. Set the format/trim, then margins (which frame and create tension), then columns and gutters, then flowlines/hanglines, then a baseline increment tied to body leading.
4. Place elements, sizing images by importance (Müller-Brockmann: "The size of the pictures is determined according to their importance for the subject").
5. Pressure-test: does the structure serve the content, or constrain it?

**Müller-Brockmann and Swiss/International Typographic Style.** *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981; his ideas first presented 1961) is the canonical text. The grid is the designer's structural framework expressing order, logic, clarity — and lends credibility: "Information presented with clear and logically set out titles, subtitles, texts, illustrations and captions will not only be read more quickly and easily but the information will also be better understood and retained." His central caveat is the key practitioner lesson: *"The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee. It permits a number of possible uses and each designer can look for a solution appropriate to his personal style. But one must learn how to use the grid; it is an art that requires practice."* The book covers grids from 8 to 32 fields plus three-dimensional grids.

**Vignelli's "right grid for the job."** *The Vignelli Canon* (2009–10, free PDF) frames the grid as the source of "intellectual elegance" and syntactic consistency, but warns it must be appropriate: "A coarse grid is restrictive, but a small grid doesn't provide enough structure." Vignelli used horizontal rules from which "type should always hang." His famous analogy: the grid is like music — "five lines and seven notes allow one to make infinite compositions." He restricted himself to six basic typefaces — Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Futura, Times, and Helvetica (his 1991 SVA exhibition used only four: Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Helvetica), on the principle that "it is not the type but what you do with it that counts." He used asymmetry and white space to create tension and dynamism within the grid.

**Breaking the grid on purpose.** Samara's *Making and Breaking the Grid* is the definitive both-sides text: understand the rules, then break them for "phenomenal cohesive layouts." Techniques: split, splice, and shift; scale shifts; overlap; optical tension; deliberate misalignment; working on an axis; spontaneous/intuitive composition; narrative constructs. The crucial discipline: breaks are "never framed as rebellious gestures for their own sake" — each break "creates emphasis, movement, or emotional charge while still maintaining an underlying order." You break the grid against the order the viewer can still sense; that residual order is what makes the break read as intentional rather than sloppy.

**Lupton & Phillips, *Graphic Design: The New Basics*** treats grid as one chapter among the formal elements (point/line/plane, scale, texture, color, figure/ground, framing, hierarchy, layers, transparency, modularity, grid, pattern, rhythm and balance) — the best single book for building reusable compositional vocabulary, with a Gestalt chapter in the 2nd edition.

### 2. Composition and the Reader's Eye

**Focal point.** Every effective layout needs one primary focal point — the entry point for attention — created through contrast, scale, color, or isolation. Without one, "all elements compete equally" and the eye wanders. Da Vinci's *Last Supper* uses converging lines to pull the eye to Christ's head — the focal point built into the composition's architecture. A focal point is the most concentrated expression of emphasis; multiple focal points work only with clear primary/secondary/tertiary ranking, else you get fragmentation. Heuristic: "If everything is emphasized, then nothing is emphasized."

**Visual hierarchy beyond type.** Rank elements using size (bigger = more important), position (top/center prominence), color and brightness (bold/contrasting colors capture attention), contrast (figure/ground separation), and isolation (white space around an element makes it a focal point). The eye "instinctively moves from most to least prominent."

**Visual flow / eye path — graded honestly:**
- **F-pattern (STRONG evidence, narrow scope):** Jakob Nielsen's 2006 NN/g study recorded how 232 users looked at thousands of web pages, identifying the F-pattern as the dominant reading pattern; NN/g re-confirmed it on desktop and mobile in 2017, including a mirrored version in right-to-left Arabic. But it is specifically a *failure mode*: it appears only when (1) text is unformatted "wall of text," (2) the user wants efficiency, and (3) they aren't deeply committed. NN/g (Kara Pernice, Nov 2017) is explicit: *"Make no mistake, the F-shaped scanning pattern is bad for users and businesses: it means that users may skip important content simply because it appears on the right side of the page."* The lesson is to design *against* it with headings, bullets, bold, front-loaded key points, and visual grouping. NN/g also documents other patterns: layer-cake (scanning headings), spotted, marking, bypassing, and commitment.
- **Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram (CRAFT HEURISTIC / OVERSTATED):** The Gutenberg diagram (primary optical area top-left → terminal area bottom-right, with "weak" and "strong fallow" areas) and the Z-pattern describe eye movement over "evenly distributed, homogeneous information" with little hierarchy. The honest verdict from practitioners: "there's little empirical evidence to support the claim" that these improve reading rhythm or comprehension, and crucially, "As soon as you create a visual hierarchy the diagram no longer applies." Treat them as starting placeholders for sparse layouts (a landing page with few elements), not laws.

The better mental model (echoing the literature): rather than forcing a layout into a named pattern, decide what you want seen and lead the eye through a deliberate sequence of focal points and flow lines.

**Balance and visual weight.**
- *Symmetrical/formal balance*: mirror across an axis; conveys stability, formality, tranquility, permanence — but can read static or "boring" because half is predictable.
- *Asymmetrical/dynamic balance* (informal balance): unequal visual weights arranged so they still balance around an implied fulcrum (heavier elements sit closer to the fulcrum, lighter ones farther). Reads as modern, energetic, dynamic; harder because there's no formula — you judge by eye. Mondrian's *Composition with Red, Yellow, Blue and Black* (1921) is the canonical study: a large red block offset by smaller blocks and black rules. Visual weight is driven by size, color, optical density, and contrast.
- *Radial balance*: elements radiate from a central point (mandalas, some logos), drawing the eye to the center.
- Practical test: squint or step back; if one area feels too dominant (and you didn't intend it), rebalance.

**Tension, movement, dynamism.** Deliberate imbalance, diagonals, and dynamic composition create energy. Tschichold's *Die neue Typographie* (1928) argued "Asymmetry is the rhythmic expression of functional design" and that asymmetry is "far more optically effective" than symmetry — and that "White space is to be regarded as an active element, not a passive background." (His later classical turn — see §6 — shows even masters treat these as choices, not laws.)

**Scale and contrast as drama.** Dramatic scale contrast — the "big-small" relationship — creates impact and hierarchy simultaneously. Lupton & Phillips devote a full chapter to scale. Müller-Brockmann treated letterforms as graphic elements and used mathematical size relationships; Vignelli: "Scale is the most appropriate size of an object in its natural context. However, it can be manipulated to achieve particular expression… by being purposely out of scale." Big-small contrast is one of the most efficient ways to make a single composition feel designed rather than defaulted.

**White space / negative space (active use).**
- *Macro* white space organizes the layout and separates major blocks; *micro* white space is the spacing within and between small elements (line spacing, padding) that drives readability.
- *Active* white space is deliberately introduced to create emphasis and flow (it's meant to be noticed); *passive* white space occurs naturally around elements to aid reading.
- Functions: creates focal points by isolation, reduces cognitive load, signals luxury/sophistication/elegance ("less is more"), and unifies a layout. The practitioner courage point: resist filling every corner; "before adding something new, ask: does this need to be here, or could space do the talking?" Generous margins give "instant elegance" and improve mobile legibility.

**Rhythm and repetition.** Repetition of elements (a recurring module, color, type treatment, or spacing interval) creates rhythm and unity across a composition and a system — the engine of consistency that lets you "carry forward" C.R.A.P.'s Repetition into multi-page work.

### 3. Type + Image Integration

**Text over image — legibility tactics (ranked toolkit):**
1. **Scrim** — a semi-transparent gradient (e.g., black fading from ~40% to transparent) behind text; ideal when text sits at one edge (a "floor fade" at the bottom is the classic).
2. **Full overlay / tint** — darken (or lighten) the whole image (~40% black) when text covers the whole frame; a brand-color overlay neutralizes a busy image.
3. **Blur** — blur the whole image or just the text's region to calm a busy background.
4. **Strip / box / highlight** — a solid or semi-transparent rectangle (e.g., 50% opacity) behind the text.
5. **Knockout** — text is cut out so the image shows only through the letters, defined by a surrounding solid color (100% legible boundaries).
6. **Drop shadow / outline** — soft shadow separates white text from background (keep subtle).
7. **Placement in calm areas** — use the image's existing quiet zones (skies, walls); "before you create contrast, can you use the existing contrast?"
8. **Heavy type** — bold/black weights survive noisy backgrounds; thin elegant serifs vanish.

**Contrast target**: aim for WCAG 4.5:1 (body) / 3:1 (large text); design for the *worst-case* pixel in the image. NN/g's core guidance: combine emotion-provoking imagery with readable text via "a semiopaque overlay… a blur, a text shadow or outline, or a combination." Note the accessibility caveat (WCAG 1.4.5): avoid text baked into images unless essential, since screen readers can't read it.

**Caption, pull quote, callout relationships.** Captions anchor to images (and are among the most-read text on a page — worth writing well); pull quotes create entry points and pacing breaks in long text; callouts direct attention to specifics. Each should relate visually to its image/text through proximity and alignment.

**Image treatment in layout.** Cropping for impact (crop tight to the focal point; remove dead area), silhouetting (cutting out the subject to interact with type and white space), full-bleed images (running off the page edge for immersion/scale), and aligning the image's internal focal point with the layout's flow. The image's focal point and the page's focal point should reinforce, not fight.

**Word + picture as one message.** Editorial and advertising practice treats type and image as a unified idea (Cath Caldwell's workshops center on "how visual language can communicate ideas simply using text and image combined"). The strongest layouts make the words and the picture say one thing together that neither says alone.

### 4. Format-Specific Craft

**Editorial / page layout.**
- The **spread** (two facing pages) is the unit of design, not the single page — design across the gutter.
- Establish a **layout system** (master grid, type styles, image treatments, repeating markers) and then **vary** it across the publication so issues feel cohesive but not monotonous.
- **Pacing** across pages is essential: Roger Black's rule #10 — *"Get lumpy! The trouble with most design is it has no surprise. If you want people to pay attention you have to change pace in your presentation."* Alternate dense text pages with open, image-led spreads.
- Balance running text, headlines, images, and white space; use the baseline grid for cross-column rhythm.
- **Read**: Cath Caldwell, *Editorial Design: Digital and Print* (3rd ed., Laurence King) — profiles Mark Porter, Scott Dadich, Janet Froelich; covers layout creation, copy/image handling, branding, and print-digital integration. Caldwell co-founded the Editorial Designers Organisation and art-directed Elle UK.

**The poster.**
- Discipline of **ONE big idea**; hierarchy legible "at a glance."
- Must work at **two distances**: impact from across the room (bold type, strong focal image, high contrast) *and* reward up close (detail, secondary info).
- A poster is a single dramatic composition — the purest exercise in focal point + scale + white space.
- **Swiss poster tradition** (Müller-Brockmann's Zurich Tonhalle/Musica Viva concert posters, Armin Hofmann at the Basel School): asymmetric layout on a grid, sans-serif type (often flush-left/ragged-right), bold contrasting (often limited) color, high-contrast objective photography, white space as a make-or-break element, "Where do my eyes go first?" as the hierarchy test.

**Social media graphics & carousels.**
- **Format**: design vertical 4:5 (1080×1350) to dominate the feed; the first slide locks the aspect ratio for the whole carousel.
- **Safe zones**: keep critical text/faces within the central ~1000×1270 area; profile grid center-crops 4:5 to a square, hiding top/bottom; keep content clear of the bottom (~250px on slide 1) where UI/dots sit; ~60px minimum edge padding.
- **First-slide hook**: the cover is the only slide seen before engagement — it must stop the scroll and create a curiosity gap. Formula: bold 5–8 word headline + high-contrast pattern interrupt + curiosity trigger. Put the *outcome* on the cover, not the topic.
- **Pacing/continuity**: "one idea per slide" (flashcard, not chapter); split dense slides; maintain a consistent template (same fonts, positions, color blocks) varying one accent per slide to build swipe momentum; use an "open loop" answered later; the **seamless panorama** (one wide canvas sliced across slides) exploits the drive for visual closure to pull swipes.
- **Sweet spot & payoff data**: 8–10 slides is the data-backed sweet spot for educational carousels (using all slides correlates with higher engagement, ~2.07%), with a specific CTA on the last slide. Per SocialInsider (Feb 2026), carousels average ~1.92% engagement vs. far lower rates for single images, and the first slide carries roughly 80% of the weight — watch completion rate in your analytics.
- Treat platform specifics as fast-moving conventions, not design principles.

**Packaging basics.**
- Designed around **3D form** and a **panel system**: front (Principal Display Panel), back, sides — the PDP carries brand + product + key claim; secondary panels carry details/story/legal.
- **Hierarchy**: a workable default order is 1) brand, 2) product, 3) variety, 4) benefit(s) — but for food the product is usually the hero; for pharma the brand may recede. Discipline: "make the product name dominant, the brand mark secondary, the key claim visible, and the legal copy quieter."
- **Shelf impact**: bold color, clear hierarchy, recognizable brand mark from several feet away; "three main visual cues are all the typical eye will tolerate." The stakes are high at the shelf: POPAI's 2012 Shopper Engagement Study (combining EEG, eye-tracking, and interviews) found an all-time-high 76% of buying decisions are made in-store — POPAI president Richard Winter urged brands to "win over shoppers where it matters most — the point of purchase." Test at 100% print size, from shopping distance (~4–6 ft for shelf, ~18 in handheld), under retail lighting.
- **Differs from flat layout**: you design for the turn of a corner, for stacking/multipack patterns, for tiny e-commerce thumbnails *and* unboxing, and within hard regulatory constraints (mandatory text x-heights, barcode quiet zones).
- **Study**: Method (minimalist hierarchy), RXBar (radical typographic transparency — ingredients as the hero), Chobani (proprietary type system).

**Other artifacts (briefly):** flyers/brochures use column/modular grids and a single dominant message; book covers are poster-logic at small scale; business cards are extreme economy of hierarchy and white space; ads fuse word+image into one idea. (Slide/presentation design is covered in a separate phase.)

### 5. Visual Sequence and Pacing

- **Pacing** is the rhythm of the reveal across multi-page/multi-slide/multi-panel pieces: alternate density and openness, vary spread/slide composition, and build a narrative arc through layout contrast.
- In **editorial**, this is the "get lumpy" principle — surprise and change of pace keep attention; a feature might open with a full-bleed image spread, move through dense text columns, and resolve on an airy closing spread.
- In **carousels**, it's hook → escalating value → payoff/CTA, with consistent templates providing continuity and one varying accent per slide providing variety.
- The universal tension: **visual variety vs. consistency** — enough repetition (system, palette, type) that the piece feels unified; enough variation (scale, density, image/text ratio) that it never feels monotonous. Repetition is the binder; pacing is the energy.

### 6. Honest Evidence Grading

**(a) STRONG — perceptual-science backing:**
- **Gestalt grouping** (proximity, similarity, closure, common region, continuity): grounded in peer-reviewed perception research. Origin: Wertheimer (1923). Modern authoritative review: Wagemans et al. (2012), "A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: I. Perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization," *Psychological Bulletin*, 138(6), 1172–1217 (DOI: 10.1037/a0029333) — confirms these principles are reviewable, quantifiable phenomena, though some sub-principles (notably *similarity*) show empirical inconsistencies.
- **Visual salience via size/luminance/color contrast** drives *early/first* fixations in free viewing: the Itti & Koch saliency model (Itti, Koch & Niebur, 1998, *IEEE TPAMI*; Itti & Koch, 2000, *Vision Research*) and empirical fixation-prediction studies (Parkhurst, Law & Niebur, 2002, *Vision Research* 42:107–123) confirm bottom-up salience predicts fixated locations better than chance — qualified by the fact that top-down task goals dominate *later* fixations.
- **Isolation effect (Von Restorff, 1933):** an item that differs from a homogeneous background is better remembered/noticed (Wallace's 1965 *Psychological Bulletin* review summarizes: "Isolating an item against a crowded or homogenous background facilitates the learning of that isolated item") — a robust behavioral effect, though the mechanism (raw perceptual salience vs. "contextual incongruity") is still debated. This underwrites "isolation creates a focal point."
- **Basic visual hierarchy via size/contrast** has support as a corollary of the above.

**(b) CRAFT CONSENSUS — well-tested professional judgment (where most of composition rightly lives):**
- Balance (symmetry/asymmetry, visual weight, the fulcrum model), white-space strategy, scale drama, type/image integration tactics, editorial pacing, poster discipline, and most compositional judgment. This is the accumulated, cross-validated wisdom of master designers (Müller-Brockmann, Vignelli, Tschichold, Samara, Lupton/Phillips). It is appropriate that this lives as craft consensus rather than lab science — it is reliable in practice without being a "law."

**(c) MYTH / OVERSTATED — treat as defaults or discard:**
- **Golden ratio and rule of thirds as aesthetic laws** (carried forward from Phase 11): useful starting defaults only. The empirical record is weak and equivocal — skeptical reviews (Green, 1995, *Perception* 24:937–968; Godkewitsch, 1974, *American Journal of Psychology*) attribute apparent golden-ratio preference to stimulus range and demand characteristics rather than intrinsic aesthetics, and modern null results dispute "the universality of a preference for the golden ratio." (Some carefully-run studies still find a slight preference, so the honest verdict is "weak and inconsistent," not "categorically false.")
- **Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram as universal reading laws**: little empirical support; apply only to sparse, hierarchy-free layouts and dissolve once real hierarchy exists.
- **The F-pattern misapplied to all layouts**: the eye-tracking evidence is real but narrow (unformatted text-heavy reading), and it describes a behavior to design *against*, not a template to impose.
- **Any rigid "rule"**: the master consensus (Müller-Brockmann, Vignelli, Samara) is that structure is an aid to be learned and then transcended, not a guarantee.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the reusable foundation (now).**
- Read in this order: Lupton & Phillips, *Graphic Design: The New Basics* (vocabulary of all formal elements); Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (grid construction); Samara, *Making and Breaking the Grid* (choosing/building/breaking grids); Vignelli, *The Vignelli Canon* (free PDF — discipline and restraint); Caldwell, *Editorial Design* (applied multi-page craft).
- For every artifact, before designing: write the *one* message, inventory content types, then pick a grid type from that inventory.

**Stage 2 — Drill the high-leverage techniques.**
- Practice creating a single clear focal point (size/contrast/color/isolation) and ranking everything else.
- Practice asymmetrical balance deliberately (place a heavy element, balance with smaller counterweights + white space; squint-test).
- Practice big-small scale contrast on posters/covers.
- Build a reusable text-over-image kit (scrim, overlay, blur, knockout) and a contrast checker into your workflow; never ship text under 4.5:1.

**Stage 3 — Build format-specific systems for the brand/side-business.**
- Editorial/brochure: a master grid + type styles + image treatments + a pacing plan (alternate dense/open).
- Social: a carousel template system (4:5, safe zones, locked positions, one varying accent) with a tested hook formula and an 8–10-slide default.
- Packaging: a panel hierarchy template (brand/product/claim/legal) and a shelf-distance + retail-lighting test.

**Benchmarks that change the recommendation:**
- If a layout feels cluttered → you likely have no focal point or too little white space; cut elements or add macro space before adding anything.
- If a text-heavy page tests poorly → you're seeing F-pattern; add headings/bullets/bold and front-load key points.
- If a carousel's completion rate drops by slide 3–4 → the hook over-promised or the middle is padded; split or cut.
- If packaging fails the squint test at shopping distance → hierarchy is too flat; make one element dominant.
- When tempted to invoke the golden ratio or rule of thirds as justification → stop; justify the decision by hierarchy, balance, and contrast instead.

## Caveats
- **Most of this is craft consensus, and that's appropriate** — composition is judgment cultivated against accumulated professional wisdom, not a set of laws. Treat named "rules" as defaults to start from and override with reason.
- **Reading-pattern models are context-dependent**: the F-pattern evidence (NN/g) is strong but narrow; Z-pattern/Gutenberg are heuristics, not laws.
- **Platform specs and engagement stats for social are volatile** (sizes, slide limits, safe zones change frequently); verify current specs at design time, and note that 2026 social-engagement figures from marketing-tool vendors (e.g., SocialInsider) are indicative benchmarks, not peer-reviewed findings.
- **Packaging carries hard regulatory constraints** (mandatory text sizes, barcode quiet zones, claims law) that override aesthetic preference and vary by market; the POPAI 76%-in-store figure is a 2012 industry study and a directional indicator of why shelf impact matters, not a timeless constant.
- **The perceptual-science core is solid but bounded**: salience predicts *early* fixations (task/top-down factors take over later), and even Gestalt grouping has inconsistent sub-principles. Don't overstate the science any more than the craft.
