# Phase 08 — Critique, Feedback & Presenting Work

## TL;DR
- The single biggest lever is reframing every conversation around **goals, not taste**: critique (and presentation, and feedback-handling) works when the work is judged against the brief's objectives rather than personal preference — this is the throughline of Adam Connor & Aaron Irizarry's *Discussing Design*, Mike Monteiro's *Design Is a Job*, and the validated psychology beneath it (Self-Determination Theory's "informational vs. controlling" feedback).
- For the friend-as-client problem specifically, the protective move is to **make it a real project on purpose** — a brief, a written scope, a defined number of revision rounds, and an explicit money conversation up front — because the danger with friends is informality, not malice.
- Grade the frameworks honestly: "I like / I wish / I wonder" and Connor & Irizarry's reactions/critique/direction distinction are genuinely useful; the **feedback sandwich is the one widely-taught technique that the evidence says to drop** (it muddies the message and reads as manipulative).

## Key Findings

1. **Critique is goal-measurement, not reaction.** Connor & Irizarry define critique as analysis of whether a design achieves its objectives; "feedback" is often just a gut reaction. Their central, most useful contribution is separating **reactions** (gut responses), **direction** (prescriptions/"make it blue"), and **critique** (analysis against goals).
2. **Self-critique is a learnable skill with concrete tools** — distance, sleep, the squint test, grayscale, horizontal flip, thumbnail, mirror — all of which artificially manufacture the "fresh eyes" that objectivity requires.
3. **Presenting is a core design skill, not an add-on** (Monteiro). Sell the thinking, not the pixels; guide the feedback; never ask "do you like it?"
4. **Vague feedback ("make it pop") is a symptom, not the disease** — it signals a real but unarticulated problem; the job is to interrogate it back to the underlying goal.
5. **Scope creep is solved structurally**, before it happens: define deliverables, set a revision-round limit in writing (2 rounds is industry standard), and route extra requests through a change order.
6. **The friend-as-client trap is informality**, not bad intent. The fix is paradoxical: be *more* structured with friends, not less.
7. **Evidence layer:** the craft consensus rests on validated psychology — autonomy-supportive/informational feedback (Deci, Koestner & Ryan), psychological safety (Edmondson), and growth mindset toward criticism (Dweck).

## Details

### 1. Design critique as a discipline

**What critique is — and isn't.** Adam Connor and Aaron Irizarry's *Discussing Design: Improving Communication and Collaboration through Critique* (O'Reilly, 2015) is the definitive practitioner text. Their framing, in Connor's own words: critique "really focuses in on how a design is or isn't achieving certain goals and framing that in a context in a dialogue… to help the creator understand those impacts," whereas plain feedback "is nothing more than just a gut reaction." Critique is *for* improving the work against agreed objectives — not asserting authority or "pushing agendas under the guise of 'feedback.'"

**The key distinction — reactions vs. direction vs. critique.** This is the single most useful idea in the book:
- **Reactions** are instinctive gut responses ("I don't like the colors") that reflect personal bias and aren't anchored to objectives.
- **Direction** is feedback that prescribes a solution ("make it blue," "move the logo up") — it jumps to an answer, often aligning the design to the giver's personal expectations.
- **Critique** is critical analysis of whether specific decisions serve the stated goals.

The practical move when you receive reactions or direction is to "ladder" back up to critique: ask *why* — what problem is this reaction or directive trying to solve? Connor & Irizarry give a five-step protocol for directive feedback: calm your reaction, take note, critique the directive (does it actually serve the goal?), critique together, move forward together.

**How to RUN a critique** (synthesizing Connor & Irizarry and Scott Berkun's "How To Run a Design Critique," Essay #23, first published 2003):
- Keep it small: **3–7 people**. Berkun, verbatim: "You can't be informal and intimate about ideas with more than 5 or 6 people in the room." Invite people likely to understand the creative process, not by job title.
- **State objectives and context first.** Define 3–4 specific questions you want answered. "Without goals everyone will work from different assumptions, and it will be more of a brainstorm than a critique."
- **Start with clarifying questions**, establishing the designer's intent, before anyone offers an opinion.
- **Listen before speaking.** Reflect back what's said before responding.
- **Avoid absolutes.** Berkun's rule: not "This sucks and it's ugly" but "If the goal is to feel friendly, black and flaming red doesn't convey that to me." Always make points referent to the goals.
- **Designer's role: present, then mostly listen.** Berkun recommends the creator lead/facilitate but warns the biggest risk is ego — using the session to fish for validation of pet ideas rather than to hear new thinking.
- **Facilitator's role:** a neutral voice manages dynamics — drawing out quiet people, quieting domineering ones, and "parking" solution-jumping ("Those are great ideas, let me note them and follow up — let's focus on analyzing what's in front of us").
- **Time-box and close the loop:** capture questions/issues, send notes and next steps afterward.
- Distinguish a **critique** (ongoing analysis to improve the work) from a **design review** (approval/sign-off). They feel similar but have different purposes; conflating them poisons critique.

**How to RECEIVE critique well.** Connor & Irizarry's steps for unsolicited/reactive feedback: be ready, hold onto your reaction, consider the source and intent, then listen and use it. Scott Berkun's blunt version: "Shut up. Just shut up and listen. Creators often fall into the trap of speaking for their work, trying to use words to defend things that should be in the design… let the prototype or draft be its own thing, and stand on its own." Practical discipline: separate self from work (see Monteiro below); listen for the *problem behind the comment*; ask clarifying questions; take notes; decide later what to act on — you don't have to promise anything in the room.

**How to GIVE good critique.** Critique against the stated goals; be specific; describe the *problem* rather than prescribing the *solution* (prescribing is "direction," which forecloses better answers); balance what works with what doesn't; prefer questions that surface alternatives over directives. Berkun on bad critics: "Bad criticism uses the opportunity provided by someone else's work to make the critic feel smart, superior or better about themselves."

**Critique frameworks — graded honestly:**

| Framework | What it is | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactions / Direction / Critique** (Connor & Irizarry) | Sorting feedback by type to convert noise into analysis | **Most useful.** The core mental model of the whole phase. |
| **"I like / I wish / I wonder (/ what if)"** (Stanford d.school) | Structured rounds: appreciation, constructive wish, generative possibility; phrased as "I-statements," no defending until everyone's spoken | **Genuinely useful**, especially for groups and non-designers. Lowers defensiveness, creates psychological safety, fast. Weakness: can stay surface-level; "I like/I wish" is still partly reaction unless tied to goals. |
| **Rose / Bud / Thorn** (design-thinking staple) | Positive (rose), potential/opportunity (bud), problem (thorn) | **Useful, lightweight**, near-identical in spirit to I like/I wish/I wonder. Good for quick retrospectives; not a deep analytical tool on its own. |
| **Plus/Delta** | What worked (+) / what to change (Δ) | **Useful and honest** — fast, low-ceremony, avoids the praise-padding problem. |
| **Six Thinking Hats** (de Bono) | Everyone examines from one "hat" (facts, emotions, risks, benefits, etc.) at a time | **Situationally useful** — depersonalizes critique ("it's the task, not me"); can feel gimmicky/heavy. |
| **Designer-led critique** | Creator frames intent and the questions they want answered, then facilitates | **Useful** — keeps the work anchored to its goals; risk is designer ego steering toward validation. |
| **The feedback sandwich** | Criticism wedged between two compliments | **Myth / drop it.** See below. |

**The feedback sandwich — the one to retire.** It is the most widely taught and the most criticized. The research consensus and practitioner critics converge: it muddies the message (recipients can't tell what matters), feels manipulative/insincere, lets people discard the criticism and keep the praise, and trains people to distrust praise (they brace for the "but"). The named evidence:
- **C.W. Von Bergen, M.S. Bressler & K. Campbell, "The Sandwich Feedback Method: Not Very Tasty," *Journal of Behavioral Studies in Business* 7 (Sept. 2014), pp. 1–13** — concludes the method "may be undermining both the supervisor's feedback and the relationship with their workers."
- **Parkes, Abercrombie & McCarty, "Feedback sandwiches affect perceptions but not performance," *Advances in Health Sciences Education* 18(3):397–407 (2013)** — students "think feedback sandwiches positively impact subsequent performance when there is no evidence that they do." (A later controlled experiment, Prochazka, Ovcari & Durinik, *Learning and Motivation* 71:101649, 2020, reaches a similar verdict.)
- Practitioner critics: Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* ("Very often the feedback sandwich is a desire to be liked or to create false harmony") and Ben Horowitz's "shit sandwich."

It was popularized by Mary Kay Ash in the 1980s. Better: separate genuine, specific praise from specific, goal-anchored critique; deliver each clearly and in a timely way. (Note: Cap Watkins has written a well-known piece partially *defending* a sandwich-like approach for relationship-building; the honest read is that the *instinct* — establish goodwill, don't open with an attack — is sound, but the rote good-bad-good structure is the part the evidence rejects.)

### 2. Self-critique and getting unstuck

The core problem is that you lose objectivity on your own work; every self-critique technique is a trick to manufacture **fresh eyes**.

**Fresh-eyes techniques (concrete, immediately usable):**
- **Sleep on it / let it rest.** Time is the most reliable tool — painters and designers consistently report that distance of a day to weeks makes flaws obvious ("When I put a painting away and return to it six months later, it's as if I never painted it").
- **View at a distance** (step back 10–20 feet) and the **squint test** — squint until detail blurs and only big shapes/contrast remain; this exposes visual hierarchy ("when you squint, you should see the most important stuff first"). If your call-to-action disappears when you squint, users will miss it too. Painters originated this; it transfers directly to logos, posters, and UI.
- **Grayscale** — remove color to test whether contrast and value structure hold up.
- **Flip horizontal / mirror** — reverses the composition so your eye stops auto-correcting and imbalance pops.
- **Reduce to a thumbnail / view at actual size / in context** — does it work at favicon scale and on the real billboard/screen?
- **Change the medium** — print it, photograph it, view on another device.

**Killing your darlings.** Borrowed from writing (Arthur Quiller-Couch, *On the Art of Writing*, 1916; popularly misattributed to Faulkner): cut the element you love most if it doesn't serve the work's goal. In design the test is rationale-based — "Only a strong rationale can save a design element from elimination… the only elements remaining will be the valuable ones." This is explicitly a **sunk-cost** discipline: the hours already spent are irrecoverable and irrelevant to whether the element earns its place now. Brandon Sanderson's honest tactic: tell yourself the lie that you'll reuse the darling elsewhere (you usually won't, because it wasn't that good — you'd just built around it).

**Getting unstuck:** step away; return to the brief/objectives (the solution is often "present in the problem," per Bierut); seek outside critique; impose deliberate constraints. Bierut's Michelangelo framing: "start with a piece of marble, then take away everything that's not the sculpture."

**Developing a designer's eye** comes from deliberate practice and studying excellent work closely — the painters' consensus that "constantly learning and growing is part of my self-critique method," studying Sargent for light, etc. Judgment is trained, not innate.

### 3. Presenting work (the communication craft)

**Presenting is a core skill, full stop.** Monteiro (*Design Is a Job*): "A designer who does not present his or her own work is not a designer… If you sit at your desk while someone else presents work to the client, you don't get to complain about the feedback. The failure was yours." And: "the hardest part of design is presenting work… Presenting is a core design skill," not an additional one.

**Structure the presentation as persuasion/story:** context/recap of the brief and goals → the strategy/thinking → the work → how it solves the stated problem → next steps. Monteiro: set the stage (thank people, tell them their role and what feedback you need, recap the prior stage and how this one moves the project forward).

**Sell the thinking, not the pixels.** Monteiro's "real estate tour" failure mode: don't narrate what they can already see ("here's the logo, here's the search box"). "You don't sell a house by talking about sheetrock. You sell it by getting the buyer to picture themselves in the neighborhood." Talk about how decisions meet the project's goals — terms the client is the expert in — not type and color, which is your domain. Bierut (*How To*, Pentagram) reinforces the same: design is storytelling; understand the context and audience; "people… make [decisions] based on stories. So find your story and tell it."

**Lead the room and the feedback loop.** Monteiro's concrete tactics: stand up and lead ("get off your ass"); don't open with an apology or disclaimer; don't read a script ("Be a scientist when you work, and a snake charmer when you present"); have someone else take notes so you can present; answer questions as questions, not as change requests ("Why is this green?" → answer it, don't say "I can change it!"). **Guide the feedback** by telling clients what kind you need: "Does this reflect your brand? Does this reflect your users' needs as we discussed in research?" — keep it in their domain of expertise. **Never ask "Do you like it?"** — it instantly converts you from hired expert to "a small child showing dad a picture of the cat," and reduces the decision to subjective taste.

**Don't "leave the price tag on."** Don't talk about how hard you worked or show the 29 hours of bad drafts; "if you did it right, it looks like it was effortless… Sell the f*** out of that one hour of good design."

**One strong direction vs. options (deeper than Phase 04).** The communication technique: present a single recommended direction *with conviction and rationale* so the client evaluates it against goals, rather than shopping among options on taste. When you must show options, Bierut's practice is to present each with its rationale and let the client react — but the default professional stance is to make a recommendation, because that's what an expert is paid for. Reference Phase 04 (Rand's one-option approach; Haviv's "never love at first sight") for the strategic case; here the *craft* is in the framing and the conviction.

**Reading the room / remote / async.** In person: walk to where you're needed, work the room, read reactions live. **Handle silence** by not rushing to fill it or over-talking — give people time; ask a guiding question. Remote/async: structured written rationale, visual annotation tools, and clear prompts substitute for body language; Connor & Irizarry note distributed critique needs explicit structure and asynchronous commentary. **Control the reveal** — don't drop unfinished work without framing; reset expectations *before* the meeting, not with an in-room apology.

**On getting defensive (the bridge to handling feedback):** Monteiro — "You are not your work and your work is not you… There's a difference between defending the work, and getting defensive. The latter is personal." The healthy posture: "It's perfectly fine to say 'That's interesting feedback. Let me think about it.'"

### 4. Handling feedback & revisions

**Translating vague feedback.** "Make it pop," "make it bigger," "make it more modern," "I'll know it when I see it" are emotional SOS signals — the client sees a real problem but lacks the vocabulary. The technique:
1. **Don't react defensively** (it produces weirder feedback or a lost client).
2. **Interrogate to the underlying goal:** "What specifically feels flat to you?" "Can you show me an example of something that 'pops' to you?" "What emotion do you want people to have?" "How do you see this serving [the audience/the brand]?"
3. **Ask for references** — competitor sites, designs they admire — to decode abstract words into concrete attributes (color contrast, hierarchy, a standout CTA).
4. **Translate into design terms and reflect back:** "When you say 'pop,' it sounds like you want the call-to-action to stand out more — is that right?"
5. **Use the pattern as prevention:** next time, guide feedback with specific questions up front ("What do you think of the typography / the amount of whitespace?").

**The core question is "what problem are we trying to solve?"** Interrogating feedback to get to the *why* behind a request is the same laddering move as converting "direction" to "critique." Monteiro: clients "will always ask you to make their logo bigger, prescribe solutions… You can roll your eyes… or you can roll up your sleeves and begin practicing your craft by helping them clarify what they need."

**Feedback that improves vs. erodes the work.** Distinguish by testing against goals, not against your ego or the client's taste. Incorporate feedback that serves the objectives; push back (with rationale) on feedback that erodes them. "Yes, and" (build on the input) is usually more productive than "yes, but" (negate it). When a directive doesn't serve the goal, critique the *directive* together rather than silently complying or flatly refusing.

**Scope creep and the endless-revision trap.** Unlimited revisions hurt both the work (it drifts, loses coherence, regresses toward committee-blandness) and the relationship (resentment, unpaid hours). Structural fixes, in order of importance:
- **Define deliverables precisely in writing.** "Build a website" invites creep; "5-page responsive site (Home, About, Services, Portfolio, Contact), 2 rounds of revisions, excludes e-commerce and copywriting" does not.
- **Set a revision-round limit. Two rounds is the industry standard** (some use three for larger projects); the exact number matters less than having one in the contract. Frame it as quality control, not restriction: "I include 2 rounds because it produces the best work — batching your feedback gives us space to think rather than react."
- **Batch feedback.** A visible "1 of 2 revisions remaining" makes clients consolidate rather than drip-feed.
- **Route extra requests through a change order** — acknowledge positively, confirm it's out of scope, quote time/cost, proceed only on approval. Script: "Love that idea — that's outside our current scope, so let me send a quick change request with cost and timeline. If you approve, I'll start right away." A 10–20% "nuisance fee" on change orders is common.

**Design by committee / conflicting stakeholders.** "A camel is a horse designed by committee." The failure mode is *no clear decision-maker* and all input weighted equally. Fixes:
- **Identify the single decision-maker** before presenting; everyone can have input, but one person owns the final call.
- **Assign roles** — decision-maker, spokesperson (consolidates a group's feedback), subject-matter expert; a RACI matrix removes ambiguity.
- **Require consolidated feedback** — one set of reconciled comments per round, with a deadline, not contradictory notes from five people at different times.
- **Consultation, not consensus** — gather input, but don't require unanimity; that's the road to diluted, generic work.

### 5. The hard conversations — and the friend-as-client dynamic

**Pushing back respectfully.** The principle: you were hired (or asked) as the expert, so behave like one — but argue from *goals and evidence*, never taste. Concrete scripts:
- Restate their feedback first ("So what I'm hearing is…") to show you listened and to surface misunderstanding.
- Make merit-based, not subjective, arguments. Go Media's William Beachy's contrast: "We shouldn't put yellow text on a white background because there isn't sufficient contrast to read it" (merit) beats "we shouldn't make your logo pink because it's ugly" (opinion).
- Offer an alternative, not just a "no": "These colors don't align with your branding, but if you want more brightness and excitement, here's how I'd do it…"
- Anchor to the audience: "Based on our user research and best practices, this approach will get the best results — here's why."
- **Disagree and commit.** Make your case fully; if the decision-maker still chooses their path after hearing your rationale and recommendation, commit to it as a partner. As 37signals' Jason Fried frames it, you "can push back as long as you have a good point of view," but you can't push back every time or you become the contrarian people route around. Document that you advised them, then execute wholeheartedly.

**The friend-as-client dynamic (the practical core).** The unique hazards: blurred boundaries, unclear expectations, money/pricing awkwardness, scope creep ("hey man, could you just…"), the difficulty of being honest with someone you love, and hurt feelings that outlast the project. The danger is *informality*, not bad intent. Strategies and scripts:

- **Make it a real project on purpose.** The protective paradox: be *more* structured with friends, not less. Use a brief, a written scope, a defined number of revision rounds, and a contract — even a simple one. Treat the friend exactly like any other client in process and professionalism. The risk Jennifer Bourn (Bourn Creative) names: because friends already love you, "you can get a bit lazy with your systems and processes… little slips, with the justification that 'they won't mind,' can add up to big client-service mistakes and frustrating conflicts."

- **Decide the money question explicitly, three legitimate options** (Jennifer Bourn): (1) **get paid in full**; (2) **discount, but with clear limits and boundaries**; (3) **work for free, but set clear expectations up front** — and in all three, treat it like a real project. "If you can't do that, it's best to say no and refer them to someone else."

- **The "what's your budget?" move** (Jon Acuff): when a friend asks for free/cheap work, respond with "What's your budget?" — "This question works because it introduces money into the equation without being offensive… The question helps your friend remember that you're a professional." Acuff's provocative position on charging friends: "Friends should pay full price… The people who love you should not try to discount your ability." And on guilt: "You do the friendship more damage when you let shame turn your no into a yes."

- **Monteiro on free work and discounts:** "Never work for free… If the situation merits it, work at a discounted rate. Submit a budget showing the actual rate, with the discount applied. Let the client know the value of what they're getting." And the contract rationale that applies even to friends: "A contract is what you need to start building trust between all parties." Monteiro warns against informal handshake agreements with friends and recommends candid conversations or a follow-up email detailing expectations to protect the relationship. (Note: a couple of these Monteiro lines circulate via reader book-notes; the substance is well-attested, but verify exact wording against the print edition of *Design Is a Job*.)

- **An itemized "fees waived" invoice** even when working free/discounted — list each item at full price with "$0.00 / no charge" next to it — so the friend sees the real value of the gift.

- **Declining a friend's project gracefully** (Jennifer Bourn): "I would love to work with you… but I like to keep my personal and professional lives separate to protect the relationships I value most… What I can do is connect you with someone I trust completely." Mark Des Cotes (Resourceful Designer): "Saying no in itself is a complete sentence. There's no need to follow it with… reasoning to justify it." A graceful exit line: say you're swamped and can't take extra work right now (just don't brag about a slow week afterward).

- **The reciprocity reframe** to recalibrate a friend's expectations: imagine asking your architect friend to "design you a house real quick," or your accountant friend to do your taxes "for a minute or two." Turning down free labor is okay.

- **Protect BOTH the work and the relationship.** Set boundaries early and in writing; separate "having a chat" from "the clock is running"; keep the friendship conversations and the project conversations distinct. The emotional labor is real — client work for people you love costs more emotional energy, which is exactly why the structure has to carry the weight instead of your goodwill.

**Saying no / declining bad ideas diplomatically / firing a client.** Decline bad ideas by reframing to goals and offering alternatives (above). Know when to walk away: Monteiro's *You're My Favorite Client* and *Design Is a Job* are explicit that not every project or client is worth taking, and that a referral both ways is valuable. With friends, the highest-integrity move is sometimes a warm "no" plus a trusted referral — protecting the friendship by *not* working together.

### 6. Evidence docking (why the craft works)

This phase is mostly **craft consensus** — the well-tested professional judgment of designers — resting on a thinner but real layer of **validated psychology**. Dock to the parent-library phases; don't re-derive:

- **Informational vs. controlling feedback (Self-Determination Theory; Deci, Koestner & Ryan, *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6):627–668, 1999 — the meta-analysis showing controlling rewards/feedback undermine intrinsic motivation while informational feedback supports competence and autonomy).** Feedback experienced as *informational* ("here's how this does/doesn't meet the goal") supports the needs for competence and autonomy and enhances intrinsic motivation; feedback experienced as *controlling* ("do this because I said so") frustrates autonomy and undermines it. This is exactly why goal-anchored critique and "describe the problem, don't prescribe the solution" work — they keep feedback informational and autonomy-supportive. **Strong evidence.**
- **Psychological safety (Amy C. Edmondson, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams," *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2):350–383, 1999).** Defined (p. 354) as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking," tested across 51 work teams in a manufacturing company. Critique only produces honest dialogue when people can speak up, disagree, and admit problems without fear — which is why facilitation, "I like/I wish/I wonder," and not-attacking-the-person matter. **Strong evidence**, reinforced by **Google's Project Aristotle (2012–2016, led by Julia Rozovsky), which studied ~180 teams and named psychological safety the most important of five dynamics of effective teams** (Google re:Work, "Understand team effectiveness").
- **Growth mindset (Carol Dweck).** Receiving criticism is where mindset shows: Dweck's own test, from "Carol Dweck Revisits the 'Growth Mindset'" (*Education Week*, Sept. 2015) — "Watch to see whether criticism brings out your fixed mindset. Do you become defensive, angry, or crushed instead of interested in learning from the feedback?" A growth orientation treats critique as information ("In a growth mindset, you look for what you've learned"), which is the disciplined receiving-stance the craft demands. **Strong on the construct; treat specific mindset-intervention claims with some caution given replication debates.**

**Evidence grading summary:**
- **STRONG (validated psychology):** informational/autonomy-supportive feedback (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999); psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999; Google Project Aristotle); specificity of feedback; growth-oriented receiving of criticism (Dweck).
- **CRAFT CONSENSUS (well-tested professional judgment):** most of this phase — how to run/give/receive critique; presentation structure and "sell the thinking"; de-vaguing feedback; revision-round structure; the friend-as-client playbook. Reliable, not lab-proven.
- **MYTH / OVERSTATED / CONTESTED:** the **feedback sandwich** (Von Bergen et al. 2014; Parkes et al. 2013 — evidence says drop it); the **"never show options" absolute** (a strong default, not a law — Bierut and others present options with rationale when warranted); heavier frameworks (Six Thinking Hats) and any "one weird trick" framing should be treated as situational, not universal.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Adopt the one mental model that does the most work.** Internalize Connor & Irizarry's **reactions / direction / critique** split. In any feedback conversation, silently classify each comment, then ladder reactions and directives back to "what goal is this serving?" Threshold to move on: you can do this reflexively, without getting defensive.

**Stage 2 — Build the self-critique habit.** Before showing anything, run a fixed checklist: sleep on it if time allows → squint test → grayscale → flip horizontal → view at actual size/in context. Keep a "darlings" graveyard file so cutting hurts less. This is free and compounds your "eye" fastest.

**Stage 3 — Upgrade how you present.** Use the structure: brief/goals recap → thinking → work → how it solves the problem → next steps. Lead with strategy; never narrate the obvious; never ask "do you like it?"; instead guide feedback with goal-anchored questions. Present one recommended direction with conviction; show options only with rationale.

**Stage 4 — Put structure in writing *before* work starts**, especially for friends. Minimum viable paperwork even for a friend: a one-paragraph brief (goal, audience, deliverables), a defined number of revision rounds (default **two**), and an explicit money decision (full / discounted-with-limits / free-with-expectations). Use a change-order line for anything out of scope.

**Stage 5 — Run the friend-as-client conversation early and warmly.** Have the money/scope/expectations talk *before* starting, using Bourn's three options and Acuff's "what's your budget?" If it can't be a real project with real boundaries, decline warmly and refer them out — protecting the friendship is a legitimate, often superior, outcome.

**Benchmarks that should change your approach:**
- If you're past **two revision rounds** and the work is drifting, stop and either invoke a change order or return to the brief — more rounds usually erode, not improve.
- If **more than one person** is giving you contradictory feedback, stop and identify the single decision-maker before continuing.
- If you catch yourself **defending rather than listening**, or feeling the criticism as personal, that's the cue to say "let me think about it" and reconvene.
- If a friend project is generating resentment or awkward silences about money, that's the signal you skipped Stage 4 — go back and formalize it, even mid-project.

## Caveats
- **Most of this is craft consensus, not lab-validated.** It's the convergent professional judgment of experienced designers (Monteiro, Bierut, Connor & Irizarry, Berkun), which is reliable but distinct from the smaller body of validated psychology it rests on. I've flagged which is which.
- **Monteiro's tone is deliberately provocative** and some of his advice ("get off your ass," confrontational client framing) suits a confident professional more than a beginner doing a favor for a friend; adapt the assertiveness to the relationship.
- **A few Monteiro quotes circulating online are from reader book-notes and quote compilations**, not verified against the print pages; the substance is well-attested but exact wording on a couple of lines should be confirmed against the books.
- **Framework popularity ≠ effectiveness.** The feedback sandwich is the clearest example; be skeptical of any technique taught mainly because it's easy to remember.
- **The "never show options" rule is contested**, not settled; treat it as a strong default that yields to context, not an absolute.
- **Friend-as-client pricing norms vary widely** by market and career stage; the structures here (contracts, revision limits, transparent discounts) transfer, but specific rates do not.
