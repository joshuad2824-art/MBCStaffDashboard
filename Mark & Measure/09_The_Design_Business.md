# Phase 09 — The Design Business: Pricing, Contracts, Clients & Ethics

## TL;DR
- **Move off the clock as fast as you responsibly can.** The strong professional consensus (Monteiro, Enns, Airey, Hische, Do) is that experienced designers price by the project or by value, not the hour — but for a beginner, a defensible flat fee built on a known break-even rate is the right starting point, with value-based pricing as the aspiration once you can credibly tie design to a client's business outcomes.
- **Two documents prevent the overwhelming majority of design-business pain: a signed contract and a clear deposit/payment schedule.** The single most expensive mistake non-professionals make is treating IP, copyright transfer, and especially *font licensing* casually — these are genuine legal liabilities, and the rule "you cannot hand your client your fonts; they need their own license" is non-negotiable.
- **Integrity is your competitive moat, not a constraint.** Honest scoping, transparent pricing, real (itemized) value even when discounting for friends, refusing spec work, and disclosing AI use aren't just ethical niceties — they map directly onto the AIGA Standards of Professional Practice and they build the referral reputation that is, in fact, how design work is actually won.

---

## Key Findings

**1. Pricing.** Three models dominate: hourly, project/flat-fee, and value-based. Hourly is transparent and easy but "punishes efficiency" (Hische) and caps your income at your available hours; it is best reserved for open-ended or undefined work. Project pricing is the practical default for most identity work — it rewards speed and gives the client certainty. Value-based pricing (Blair Enns) prices a percentage of the value the work creates for the client's business and can multiply fees, but it is genuinely hard for beginners and is contested as overhyped/hard to operationalize. Every designer should still know their **break-even rate** via the freelance-rate calculation, because it is the floor beneath any pricing model.

**2. The paperwork.** A proposal is a sales/closing tool; a contract protects both parties *and the relationship*. The essential clauses: scope, deliverables, timeline, payment terms, revision-round cap, kill/cancellation fee, IP/copyright transfer on final payment, usage rights, indemnity, confidentiality, and source-file terms. Free, respected templates exist: the **AIGA Standard Form of Agreement** (modular, lawyer-drafted) and **Andy Clarke's "Contract Killer"** (plain-language).

**3. IP & ownership.** Under US law the *creator* owns copyright automatically; the client only gets what a signed writing transfers. "Work made for hire" generally does *not* apply to independent contractors except for narrow statutory categories, so the correct mechanism is a written **copyright assignment that vests on final payment**. This is a frequent dispute source and must be explicit in the contract.

**4. Font licensing.** The single biggest hidden liability in client work. A font EULA licenses *you*, on *your* machines, for defined uses. You generally cannot give the client your font files — they need their own license. Desktop, web, app, and broadcast are separate licenses. Outlining the logo's lettering to vectors removes the need for the client to license the font *for the logo itself*, but any ongoing typesetting of brand materials requires the client's own license.

**5. Getting & keeping clients.** Referrals and word-of-mouth dominate (Monteiro: referrals are the source of the large majority of work). Specialization/niching gets you paid more (Enns, Philip Morgan). A portfolio and personal brand are your main acquisition engine; ethically-done friend/pro-bono projects are legitimate portfolio seeds.

**6. Ethics.** The profession opposes spec work (AIGA, No!Spec). AIGA's Standards of Professional Practice codify honesty, confidentiality, conflict-of-interest disclosure, fair fees, and no hidden kickbacks. AI use in design is genuinely unsettled — current US law denies copyright to purely AI-generated work, and honest disclosure of AI use is the emerging (but contested) professional norm.

## Details

### 1. PRICING

#### The three models

**Hourly.** You charge for time spent. Pros: simple, transparent, low risk on open-ended work, easy to justify. Cons: it caps income at billable hours, "punishes efficiency" (the better you get, the less you earn for the same result — Jessica Hische, *The Dark Art of Pricing*), invites clock-watching and sticker-shock, and turns you into a comparable commodity (clients compare hourly rates detached from value). Best use: undefined scope, ongoing maintenance, "difficult client" insurance, or work where you genuinely can't predict the shape.

**Project / flat-fee.** A single fixed fee for a defined scope. Pros: client certainty, rewards your efficiency, decouples pay from hours, easier to sell. Cons: you carry the estimation risk — underestimate scope and you eat the loss; and like hourly it can still be compared head-to-head with a competitor's number. This is the pragmatic default for logo/identity work and the right model for a side-business starting out.

**Value-based (Blair Enns, *Pricing Creativity: A Guide to Profit Beyond the Billable Hour*, 2018).** Price on the value the work delivers to the client's business — "not based on the inputs of time and material, not based on the market value of the outputs of say the logo, but based on the value to the client of that logo" (Enns). Enns's framework: identify the client's objectives, identify the KPIs/measures of success, identify the value that might be created, then set price using a guide that includes asking what the client would be willing to pay across a broad range. A signature Enns tactic is offering **three tiered options** anchored by a "what would we do if money were no object?" top tier, which raises the reference point for the whole negotiation. The principle "the sooner in the sale you offer a price, the lower it is likely to be" underpins his insistence on the *value conversation* before any proposal.

**Why experienced designers move off hourly.** Both Hische and Enns make the same core argument: hourly ties your income to a finite resource (time) and penalizes the expertise that lets you work faster, while value/project pricing lets a great designer be paid for the *result*, not the clock. Monteiro's framing in *Design Is a Job*: "charge for value provided, rather than simply for time worked," and the real barrier isn't knowing what to charge — "it's that they're afraid to charge it."

**Evidence grade:** The *superiority* of value-based pricing is CRAFT/INDUSTRY CONSENSUS bordering on CONTESTED. Real practitioners push back: one 17-year freelancer (writing at solowork.co) still prices hourly, "making a darn good living," and argues value-based pricing "sounds great in theory" but is hard to operationalize with real clients, especially when goals are emotional/brand-oriented rather than a clean revenue number. Pricing-discipline academics note implementing value-based pricing fails for "cognitive and organizational rather than analytical" reasons — "realizing the VBP was much harder than calculating value-based prices." **Verdict: learn the value conversation, but don't expect to run a pure value-based practice as a beginner.**

#### The freelance-rate / break-even calculation (STRONG, do this first)

Even committed value-pricers need their floor. The AIGA method ("Calculating a Freelance Rate"):
1. **Total annual business expenses** (software, hardware amortization, insurance, workspace, marketing, professional development) + **a realistic salary for yourself**.
2. **Realistic billable hours** — *not* 2,080. The classic error is dividing a target salary by 2,080 (40 hrs × 52 wks). Real freelancers bill only 50–70% of working hours; the rest is admin, marketing, and sales. New freelancers often bill closer to 50–60%.
3. **Add taxes** — self-employment tax is 15.3% on top of income tax; budget 25–35% of gross for all taxes combined.
4. **Divide total need by billable hours = break-even rate.** Then add a profit margin (~20%).

Worked example (composite of the calculators surveyed): a $75,000 take-home target with ~$15,000 expenses and ~1,300 billable hours, after a ~35% effective tax adjustment, requires roughly **$83/hour** — versus the naïve $46/hr you'd get from $60k ÷ 1,300, which would leave you ~$37,000 short. The lesson the calculators repeat: most freelancers undercharge because they forget taxes, non-billable time, and benefits an employer used to cover. For a **day rate**, multiply the hourly by ~6–7 hours, not 8, to account for meeting overhead.

**Benchmarks (caveat: vary widely by market, region, and date):** Freelance graphic designers commonly cited at **$40–$125/hr**; agencies bill **$75–$175/hr** with project minimums effectively setting a floor around $1,500–$3,000. US-based coastal rates run higher; Southeast Asian rates can be 40–60% lower.

#### What logo/identity work actually costs (graded ranges, present with caveats)

Prices span five-plus orders of magnitude because "logo design isn't a commodity with fixed inputs and outputs" — you pay for strategy, experience, revisions, rights, and overhead, not a picture file.

- **DIY / AI tools** (Canva, Looka, Wix, Fiverr Logo Maker): **$0–$100**. Templates, limited rights, originality risk.
- **Freelance marketplace / entry** (Fiverr/Upwork tiers): **~$5–$500**. Custom-ish, minimal strategy, file/communication risk.
- **Experienced freelance / solo studio:** **~$300–$2,500+**, with strong independents confidently at **$1,000–$2,000+**. Multiple guides put the realistic small-business sweet spot at **$300–$2,000**.
- **Boutique branding studio:** **~$3,000–$25,000+** for a logo-plus-identity system (cited as £3,000–£25,000 by one UK studio guide).
- **High-end agency:** **$10,000–$50,000–$100,000+** (full identity systems). For scale of the extreme top end: the London 2012 Olympics logo, designed by Wolff Olins, cost £400,000 — former Wolff Olins chairman Brian Boylan led the £400,000 project, and it has been recognized as the most expensive Olympic logo.

**The crucial insight (and the honest caveat):** price in 2026 reflects *overhead, location, and business model* more than design quality. As one studio guide put it, "A £500 freelancer working from a solid brief can outperform a £15,000 agency padded with account managers" — "the real quality signal is process, not price." Don't equate your price with your worth, and don't assume a high price guarantees quality. (Phase 04 covers the discovery/brief process that drives this value; not repeated here.)

#### Friends-and-family pricing (the "mates rates" trap)

This is where money and relationship collide. The mechanics (Phase 08 covers the interpersonal dynamic; here's the money discipline):

- **Decide your policy in advance and write it down.** Either treat them as full clients, or have a defined friends-and-family discount (commonly cited as **10–20%**), or do it free *deliberately*. What you must not do is improvise.
- **The resentment mechanism is real and predictable.** Discounting signals that the discounted rate is your true value; and every below-rate hour is income lost if it displaces a full-paying client. "Don't turn a fully paying client down in favour of mates rates work. It'll breed resentment."
- **Always issue an itemized invoice showing full value, then the discount.** Best practice: list each service at full price, then write "fees waived"/"no charge"/"$0.00" beside it, so the friend sees the real value of the gift. This is the single most important money-mechanic for friend work.
- **Protect your rate with confidentiality.** Put the discount under an NDA-style clause and ask friends to quote your *full* rates when referring you — otherwise every referral arrives expecting "mates rates."
- **Still sign a contract.** A discount is not a reason to drop professionalism; the contract protects the friendship by making expectations explicit.
- **Sustainable rule of thumb:** discount only if you remain profitable and aren't sacrificing higher-paid work; if you're at capacity, it's fine to say so and offer the full rate or a later slot.

#### Deposits, payment schedules, kill fees, late fees, getting paid

- **Deposit:** require **25–50% upfront** before work begins; **50% is standard** for new clients and smaller projects (<$5k). "A client who won't pay a deposit isn't a client worth having." This is universal professional practice, not paranoia.
- **Milestone payments** for larger projects: e.g., deposit on signing (30–50%), payment at design approval/midpoint (20–30%), final on delivery (20–30%). **The 25% rule:** never have more than ~25% of the total fee outstanding at final delivery — that's your maximum acceptable exposure.
- **Kill / cancellation fee:** standard in motion/identity work. Example clause: "If the project is cancelled or terminated before the first round of sketches are approved, a kill fee of 50% is due; if cancelled after first-round sketches are approved, the full fee is due." Monteiro recommends a commencement fee/deposit and an extra charge if the client *pauses* the project.
- **Late fees:** commonly **1.5% per month** (or 1–2% monthly interest) on overdue balances; must be stated in the contract and reasonable enough to defend in court. Add as a clear line item. Net-14 or Net-7 terms increasingly replace Net-30 for small clients.
- **Getting paid (Monteiro):** IP transfers on full payment — *that's your leverage*. Most late payments are non-malicious, so chase politely first. Keep contract and SOW separate (one master contract can cover multiple SOWs). It's OK to have a lawyer.

### 2. THE PAPERWORK / LEGAL

#### Proposals
A strong proposal doubles as your closing tool. It should define scope precisely (deliverables, what's *not* included), establish your understanding of the client's goals, present pricing (ideally tiered, anchored high per Enns), set timeline and the client's responsibilities, and state terms. Enns's discipline: do the value conversation and "go for the 'no' early" — "No's early are your friends. When you hear them late, they are your enemies."

#### The contract — why and the clause checklist
Monteiro's case (*Design Is a Job*): "Contracts protect both parties. Don't start work without a contract." A contract isn't a sign of distrust; it's how professionals make expectations explicit and protect the *relationship* when circumstances change (market shifts, your contact leaves, the project goes rogue). The moment you write an emotional letter appealing to guilt instead of pointing to a contract, "you have given up… you have become a bottom in that relationship."

**Clause checklist (composite of Monteiro, Contract Killer, AIGA Standard Form):**
- Scope of work / deliverables (and explicit exclusions)
- Timeline + client responsibilities ("you'll give us the assets and info we need, when we ask, in the formats we need" — Contract Killer)
- Payment terms: deposit, schedule, late fees
- **Revision rounds capped** (commonly 2–3); work beyond the cap billed hourly. The AIGA form ties "substantive changes" to a percentage-of-time threshold.
- Kill/cancellation fee
- **IP / copyright transfer on final payment**; usage rights vs. full assignment
- Source-file terms (whether included, and price if sold separately)
- Confidentiality / NDA
- Indemnity — Monteiro's caution: *don't* indemnify the client (you could be held liable for their losses), and don't guarantee the design will meet business goals ("the client has a goal; you provide the plan to get there")
- Single point of contact; "bringing in another designer is a fireable offense" (Monteiro)
- Right to display the work in your portfolio

**Templates:**
- **AIGA Standard Form of Agreement for Design Services** (updated 2020/2022): modular, lawyer-drafted (IP attorney Linda Joy Kattwinkel + Shel Perkins). Two core modules (Basic Terms & Conditions; Intellectual Property Provisions) for every job, plus Print/Interactive/Environmental supplements. Free; you attach your own custom proposal as the project-specific part.
- **Andy Clarke's "Contract Killer"** (originally 2008, repeatedly updated; widely mirrored on Docracy/GitHub): plain-language, peer-reviewed web/design contract. Clarke's thesis: contracts can be written in plain English; "no one has ever refused to sign it" and the clarity gets contracts signed *faster*. Covers scope, payments, revisions, rights, and "what happens if things change."

#### IP & ownership (a real dispute source)
- **Default US law:** the creator owns copyright the instant work is fixed; the client owns only the *deliverable* (the file), not the bundle of copyrights, absent a signed transfer. Section 204 of the Copyright Act requires copyright transfers to be **in writing and signed**.
- **Work made for hire** vests authorship in the employer/commissioner *from creation* — but for independent contractors it only applies to nine narrow statutory categories *and* requires a pre-creation signed agreement. You **cannot** create work-for-hire just by writing the words into a contract if the work isn't one of those categories. So the reliable route for freelance logo work is a **copyright assignment that transfers on final payment** (many designers also retain rights to rejected/unused concepts and to display work in their portfolio).
- **Usage license vs. full assignment:** selling *usage rights* (license) is narrower and cheaper; *assignment* transfers ownership and costs more because the creator permanently loses future licensing. The contract must state which. A client who wants to trademark the logo, modify it, or resell it needs full assignment.
- **Trademark vs. copyright (brief):** copyright protects the artwork; trademark protects the mark as a brand identifier in commerce. A client should run a trademark clearance search before filing (the AIGA form puts clearance responsibility on the client). Note that purely AI-generated marks face copyright/trademark-eligibility problems (see Ethics).
- **Source files:** you're not obligated to hand over native/working files unless the contract says so. A commonly cited practice is charging a premium (e.g., ~300% of original cost) for native files if sold; many designers decline. Decide and state it upfront.

The creative brief is part of the agreement (defined scope flows from it — see Phase 04).

### 3. CLIENT ACQUISITION & MANAGEMENT

**Getting work.** Monteiro: referrals are the source of the overwhelming majority of work; for RFPs, build a relationship with the person behind it to bypass the cattle-call; don't cold-call — go through your network; advertising at most makes you *familiar*; conferences build peers and prospects. Your **portfolio and personal brand** are the engine; writing/teaching (as Airey and Morgan both demonstrate) compounds visibility.

**Positioning / niching (the specialist-gets-paid-more argument).** Philip Morgan (*The Positioning Manual for Indie Consultants*): specialization yields "visibility, momentum, impact, and profit"; "specialists are able to ask for and receive more financial compensation… than those without any specialized expertise." Enns's first proclamation: "Expertise is the only valid basis for differentiating ourselves from the competition. Not personality. Not process. Not price." The mechanism: fewer substitutes for your expertise → more power in the relationship → higher fees and the ability to refuse spec work. Airey's blunt version: "we don't need another generalist." For a side-business, even a *soft* niche (an industry you know, a deliverable you're best at) beats "I do everything."

**Onboarding & management.** Set expectations explicitly at kickoff; establish a single point of contact and a communication cadence; use status updates; hold boundaries (the contract is your backstop). Monteiro: "Be pleasant, not nice. Clients hire you to solve problems, not be their friend." Good clients are "open to your solutions… they aren't committed to their own solutions."

**Red flags & qualifying (decline these).** Concrete warning signs, consolidated from freelance-business sources (Morgan Overholt, Devlin Peck, Colleen Gratzer/Design Domination, David Airey, et al.):
1. Haggling on price before understanding value ("What's your absolute lowest price?").
2. "This'll be quick/simple/easy" — near-guaranteed scope creep.
3. "Lots of future work" promised in exchange for a discount now.
4. Payment in "exposure" or "great for your portfolio."
5. Refusing to share a budget range (often a prelude to lowballing).
6. Requests for spec/free sample work (increasingly disguised as "show us your approach first").
7. **Telling *you* how long the work should take** — undermines your expertise.
8. Disrespecting your process or treating design as "just making it pretty."
9. Scope vagueness — can't articulate what they want well enough to brief.
10. A trail of fired previous designers / "our last three couldn't capture our vision."
11. Bad-mouthing previous collaborators (they were likely part of the problem).
12. Refusing to sign a contract — "ask yourself why."
13. Refusing a deposit.
14. Ghosting/poor communication while still demanding deadlines.
15. Expecting 24/7 availability; no boundaries.
16. Excessive unpaid pre-work (endless calls/interviews/training before any paid assignment).
17. No clear decision-maker.
18. Your own gut ("if something feels off"). On saying no, see Phase 08.

### 4. DELIVERABLES & HANDOFF (business/licensing side)

(Technical file formats/production = Phase 10.) As a *business* matter, the client typically receives: final logo files in the agreed formats and lockups, a color/typography specification, and — increasingly expected — a **brand guidelines / style guide** as a deliverable. Source/native files are a separate negotiated item (above). Confirm usage rights in writing before final payment.

#### Font licensing — the professional liability (thorough, practical)
This is the area where well-meaning amateurs and even agencies get burned, with real financial penalties.

**The core rule:** when you "buy" a font you buy a **license to use the software**, not ownership of the design; the foundry retains copyright. The EULA defines and limits use. **Critically: your license covers *your* machines and *your* usage. If you buy a font for a client project and hand over the files, the client almost always needs their own license.** Multiple foundry EULAs say this explicitly — e.g., Otherwhere Collective: "If You have purchased a license… to test Fonts for design development on behalf of a client, the client must buy their own license… as soon as they use or share designs using the Fonts." Grilli Type: "you can't transfer the license to your client or anyone else."

**License types are separate purchases:**
- **Desktop** — installing the font to create files/print; covers "just about anything you create on your computer." Distributing desktop-licensed fonts to a client is "strictly illicit."
- **Web** — `@font-face` embedding on a website; priced by monthly pageviews; does **not** cover apps.
- **App** — embedding in application software; usually per-app, priced by downloads/users.
- **Broadcast / e-pub / server** — further separate tiers.
- *Nuance:* a **static rasterized image** of type used on the web only needs a *desktop* license, not a webfont license — a common misconception.

**The outlining nuance (critical for logos):** converting the logo's lettering to **vector outlines** is "generally the safest way to provide the final artwork to a client without requiring them to own a separate license" — *for the logo itself*. But this does **not** license the client to typeset their own brand materials (business cards, website body copy, signage) in that typeface; for ongoing use **the client must buy their own license**. Read EULAs even on outlining/merchandise — a standard desktop license may not cover mass-producing physical goods.

**The financial risk is concrete.** Foundries (notably Monotype) actively audit usage and detect domains using the wrong tier. Documented patterns: a freelancer shared raw font files for "minor edits," the client used the font in a national campaign, and *both* received demand letters because the EULA prohibited sublicensing; an agency used a desktop license on a high-traffic site and was billed for the correct web tier *plus back-dated infringement penalties*. Some EULAs set fixed penalties — the Grilli Type EULA states: "The penalty for breach of the terms and conditions of this EULA is half of the total amount of the license in question, but is always at least US$ 4,000.00. If you have used the Fonts without having purchased a license, the full amount of the license costs in question will be added to the penalty payment." **Practical protocol:** never distribute raw font files; match the license to the medium (desktop for logo creation, web for sites, app for apps); register the *client* as the licensee when buying on their behalf; keep invoices/an audit trail; and prefer transparent-licensing foundries or subscription routes (Adobe Fonts via Creative Cloud, with the caveat that fonts stop working if the subscription lapses — a dependency your brand guidelines must note).

#### Stock assets, icons, photography
Same principle: licenses don't automatically transfer with the design files. Confirm that every stock image, icon, and photograph used in client work carries a license that permits the client's intended use (commercial, scale, modification), and that the license is in the *client's* name where ongoing use requires it. DIY/template tools often bundle elements whose commercial-use rights are restricted — verify before delivery.

### 5. POSITIONING & GETTING CLIENTS AS A SMALL PRACTICE

- **Building a portfolio from zero:** friend and pro-bono projects are legitimate portfolio pieces *if done ethically* — real brief, real engagement, full professionalism, and (per AIGA) understanding that unpaid work done deliberately as a favor, for experience, or "for the public good" is categorically different from spec work. AIGA encourages designers to contribute ~5% of time to public-good projects. Volunteer for a cause and do the *whole* identity, not one-off flyers — it shows strategic range.
- **Niche/specialization** (above): the indie designer's highest-leverage decision. Morgan's nuance: emergent niches have little competition and reward you for publishing how-to content as marketing, but platform/tech niches commoditize over ~7 years, so revisit positioning periodically.
- **Pricing confidence & raising rates:** undercharging is the default error. Raise rates for new clients first; give existing clients 30–60 days' notice framed as a market adjustment; aim for ~10–20% annual increases; the best time to raise is when you're fully booked. Airey: raising rates over time "benefits your clients" by keeping your practice sustainable and engaged.
- **Sustainable side-hustle practice:** don't undercharge (it caps quality and breeds resentment and burnout); be selective (Enns: "By using 'no' we will give power and credibility to our 'yes.'"); a business is only a business if it turns a profit.

### 6. PROFESSIONAL ETHICS

**Spec work & the No!Spec movement.** Spec work = work done *before* engagement in the hope of being paid if selected (including design contests/crowdsourcing). AIGA's position: "professional designers should be compensated fairly for the value of their work" and should negotiate ownership/use rights through a genuine engagement; instead of working speculatively, "enter into projects with full engagement." AIGA distinguishes spec work from legitimately uncompensated work (volunteer, internship, pro bono). The arguments against spec: clients get compromised quality (no research/iteration), designers are exploited and the profession devalued, and there are real IP/trademark risks for both parties. AIGA provides a sample client letter explaining why. **Evidence grade:** the arguments are CRAFT CONSENSUS; "never do spec, ever" as an *absolute* is somewhat CONTESTED — AIGA itself softened from a flat prohibition to an education/individual-choice stance circa 2009, recognizing the market has changed, and the practice persists (the "No Free Pitches" petition, 6,000+ signatories, shows it's still live and contested). Architecture, advertising, and broadcast have different conventions.

Enns's deeper reframe in *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* (2010) — structured as twelve "proclamations" including *We Will Specialize*, *We Will Replace Presentations With Conversations*, *We Will Diagnose Before We Prescribe*, *We Will Be Selective*, *We Will Address Issues of Money Early*, *We Will Refuse to Work at a Loss*, and *We Will Charge More* — is that free pitching gives away your "highest value product": "Under no circumstances will we part with our thinking without appropriate compensation, for our thinking is our highest value product and if we demonstrate that we do not value it, our clients will not." His pitch definition: attempting "to sell or win approval for one's ideas by giving them away for free, usually within a competitive, buyer-driven process." Power, in his model, rests with whoever has alternatives — so specialization (being seen as a narrowly focused expert who *diagnoses before prescribing*, like a doctor, rather than a presenter who persuades) removes the client's ability to demand free spec work. Two related Enns maxims worth internalizing: "Those who cannot talk money, do not make money" (the Rule of Money), and on being selective, "By using 'no' we will give power and credibility to our 'yes.'"

**AIGA Standards of Professional Practice (the profession's code).** Key duties: act in the client's best interest within professional limits; avoid undisclosed conflicts of interest; keep work-in-progress and client information confidential; don't supplant another designer by unethical inducement or take over another's live project without proper notice; criticize peers' work fairly; **work only for an agreed fee/royalty/salary and retain no hidden kickbacks, discounts, or commissions — and disclose mark-ups to the client**; disclose any financial interest in suppliers you recommend. These map almost one-to-one onto ordinary commercial integrity.

**AI & design ethics (genuinely unsettled — flag as evolving).** AI use in design is now near-universal: the *AI in Design 2026* report by Designer Fund and Foundation Capital (a survey of 900+ designers across 60+ countries) found "weekly AI usage for design tasks has jumped from 54% to 91%, and 75% use it daily." The hard facts: the **US Copyright Office denies copyright to purely AI-generated work** lacking meaningful human authorship; "significant human creative input" is being litigated case-by-case. The governing example is the US Copyright Office Review Board's September 5, 2023 decision on Jason M. Allen's *Théâtre D'opéra Spatial* (a 624-prompt Midjourney image): "If all of a work's traditional elements of authorship were produced by a machine, the work lacks human authorship and the Office will not register it." Practical implications: a purely AI-generated logo may offer **no legal protection** against copying and may be un-trademarkable without substantial human authorship; document your creative direction (prompts, iterations, post-processing) if you want to claim authorship; tools trained on unlicensed data carry infringement risk (Adobe Firefly offers commercial indemnification as a safer route). The **ethics of disclosure** — telling clients when and how you used AI — is the emerging norm but is **contested and not settled**; honesty here is both prudent (legal risk) and consistent with the integrity standard. Present this section to readers as a live, changing area.

**Integrity in pricing & dealings (the quiet root).** The throughline of every reputable source — Monteiro, Enns, Airey, AIGA — is that honest dealing is also good business: honest estimates (no padding), transparent pricing, doing what you say, delivering itemized value even when you discount, refusing kickbacks, and being a faithful steward of the client's money and trust. This is simply universal professional integrity — honest weights and measures, treating clients fairly, keeping your word — and it happens to align with a Christian ethic of stewardship and honesty without needing to be announced. It is, practically, what generates the referrals that *are* how the work gets won.

**Firing a client / walking away.** Sometimes the ethical move is to end the relationship. Enns: "When we express our resentment for the client who does not value us, we are really expressing our self-loathing for not being able to walk away from him." Do it cleanly per the contract's termination/kill-fee terms, hand over what's been paid for, keep what hasn't, and stay professional. (Interpersonal mechanics in Phase 08.)

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Before your next project (the non-negotiable foundation):**
1. **Calculate your break-even rate** using the AIGA method with *realistic* billable hours (assume 50–60% to start) and a 25–35% tax reserve. This is your floor for every quote.
2. **Adopt a contract today.** Start from the AIGA Standard Form (core two modules) or Contract Killer; have a lawyer review your master version once. Never start work without it.
3. **Default to project/flat-fee pricing** with a **50% deposit** for new clients and a **revision cap of 2–3 rounds**, beyond which you bill hourly.
4. **Write a one-page friends-and-family policy**: your discount (e.g., 10–20% or "deliberately free"), itemized-invoice-showing-full-value rule, confidentiality on rate, and that a contract still applies.

**Stage 2 — As you take on real client work:**
5. **Solve font licensing every single time.** Build a per-project checklist: which fonts, what mediums (desktop/web/app), is the *client* the licensee, is the logo outlined, do they need their own license for ongoing materials. Never ship raw font files. Keep the audit trail.
6. **Put IP transfer in writing, vesting on final payment.** Use copyright *assignment* language (not just "work for hire") and decide your source-file and rejected-concept terms upfront.
7. **Qualify clients against the red-flags list** on a discovery call; require a budget range before you write a proposal; "go for the no early."

**Stage 3 — Growing the side-business:**
8. **Pick a soft niche** (industry or deliverable) and publish about it; specialization is your highest-leverage move for both visibility and fees.
9. **Raise rates ~10–20%/year**, new clients first, ideally when fully booked.
10. **Refuse spec work**; do public-good/pro-bono work *deliberately* and use it as portfolio (ethically). Disclose AI use; document authorship; prefer commercially-indemnified AI tools if you use them.

**Benchmarks that should change your approach:**
- If you're billing **<break-even rate**, stop and reprice immediately — you're subsidizing clients.
- If **>25%** of a project's fee is unpaid at delivery, restructure your milestones.
- When you're **consistently fully booked**, that's the signal to raise rates and/or shift selected projects toward value-based pricing.
- When a client triggers **2+ red flags**, decline or price in the risk.
- When you can **credibly tie your work to a client's revenue/business outcome**, you're ready to pilot value-based pricing on that project.

## Caveats
- **All pricing numbers vary widely by market, region, discipline, and date,** and several ranges here come from vendor/marketplace guides (Fiverr, ManyPixels, Vistaprint, etc.) with an interest in framing cost — treat them as indicative ranges, not authorities. The AIGA salary/freelance-rate material and documented copyright/EULA facts are the STRONG evidence; most pricing-practice guidance is CRAFT CONSENSUS.
- **Value-based pricing is championed and criticized;** it is genuinely hard for beginners and may not suit emotional/brand goals. Don't treat it as a solved technique.
- **The AIGA|Aquent Survey of Design Salaries** is the most authoritative US compensation dataset, but recent editions are member-gated and somewhat dated; AIGA itself notes design compensation has lagged inflation for years. Salary aggregator figures (ZipRecruiter/Salary.com "AIGA Design" entries) are *not* the survey and shouldn't be cited as such.
- **Legal specifics are US-centric** (Copyright Act §§101/204, work-for-hire). Other jurisdictions differ; confirm locally and get a lawyer for your master contract.
- **AI law and ethics are moving fast** and are contested across jurisdictions — anything here is a June 2026 snapshot.
- This phase deliberately defers **technical file/production detail to Phase 10** and the **interpersonal client/critique dynamics to Phase 08**.
