# Constitution, bylaws, rules of order, and standing rules — what each one does

**Short answer:** Robert's sorts an organization's rules into four kinds, from hardest to change to easiest:

1. **The constitution:** the essentials
2. **The bylaws:** important rules that need notice to change
3. **The rules of order:** how meetings run
4. **Standing rules:** practical arrangements a majority can adopt at any meeting

A lower rule that conflicts with a higher one is void.

## The four kinds

**Constitution.** Robert's says it should hold only:

- the name and purpose
- who can be a member
- the officers and how they're elected
- the essentials about meetings
- how to amend it

Nothing in it should be suspendable. Changing it should take notice and a two-thirds or three-fourths vote.

**Bylaws.** These hold every other rule important enough that it shouldn't change without notice: officers' duties, committees, the quorum, how to amend. Bylaws can't be suspended unless they say they can. The exception is bylaws that are really rules of order, about how business is done. Those can be suspended by two-thirds like any rule of order.

**Rules of order.** These cover how meetings run and what the officers do. Robert's advises adopting a standard parliamentary manual as the authority and adding only the special rules the group actually needs. He even gives the clause to adopt: the manual "shall govern the society in all cases to which [it is] applicable, and in which [it is] not inconsistent with the by-laws or the special rules of order of this society."

**Standing rules.** These are practical, non-procedural rules, like what time meetings start in the summer. A majority can adopt them at any meeting without notice, and a majority can suspend them for a meeting. Amending or rescinding one takes two-thirds, or a majority with notice.

**Which wins.** The order runs: law, then constitution, then bylaws, then rules of order, then standing rules and resolutions. A motion that conflicts with any of them is out of order (see [Votes that don't count](votes-that-dont-count.md)).

## At MBC

MBC's documents don't line up neatly with Robert's four categories, so here's how they compare:

- **Constitution.** It names the church and its incorporation. It adopts the Baptist Faith & Message 2000 as its Articles of Faith and sets out the Church Covenant (Constitution, Arts. I–III).
- **Bylaws.** Articles I–VII cover membership, leadership, programs and committees, meetings, amendments, scope, and affiliation. Art. VI says the bylaws serve both the unincorporated church and the Oklahoma religious corporation.
- **Parliamentary authority.** Our bylaws adopt Robert's as "a general guide to parliamentary procedure except for changes specifically identified by these by-laws" (Art. IV §2 ¶1). That's close to the clause Robert's recommends. Two differences: no edition is named, and "general guide" is looser than "shall govern." Neither document defines that difference. This reading is worth confirming with church leadership.
- **Policies.** The Deacon Board is charged with "formulating general policies for the church" (Art. II.B §3 ¶5), and the policy manual holds the results. Robert's has no category called "policies." Some function like standing rules, and some are close to rules of order. The corpus doesn't set a single procedure for adopting or amending them. That's a gap, not a rule.
