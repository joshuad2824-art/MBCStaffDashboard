# Chairing a meeting

**Short answer:** The chair's job is to keep the group moving through its business fairly. That means announcing each item, recognizing speakers, stating every motion clearly, taking and announcing every vote, and saying what comes next. Robert's says the chair of a large group should have the least to say of anyone on the merits of what's being decided.

## The chair's duties

1. **Open on time** and call the meeting to order.
2. **Announce the business in order** (see [Order of business](order-of-business.md)).
3. **Recognize members** who are entitled to the floor (see [Getting the floor](getting-the-floor.md)).
4. **State every motion and put it to a vote,** then announce the result, what it means, and what's next (see [Motion, start to finish](how-a-motion-moves.md)).
5. **Keep discussion within the rules,** and keep order and courtesy.
6. **Decide points of order,** subject to appeal (see [Point of order](point-of-order-and-appeal.md)).
7. **Answer procedural questions** that bear on the business at hand.
8. **Protect the group from stalling** (see [Stalling motions](dilatory-motions.md)).
9. **Sign the group's acts and orders** when needed.

**In an emergency** like a fire or serious disorder, the chair can declare the meeting adjourned to another time without a vote, if a vote isn't practical or delay would be dangerous.

## How the chair carries it

- **Speak as "the chair,"** not "I." "The chair rules that the amendment is in order."
- **Stand to put a vote,** except in small boards and committees. Sit and listen during discussion.
- **Stay out of the debate.** Robert's says a chair who even looks partisan loses some of their ability to keep order. Anyone who expects to take an active part in a debate shouldn't take the chair for it. If the chair does step down to speak, they shouldn't return until that question is settled. Robert's adds that this "should rarely be done," and never when feelings are running high.
- **Vote only when it changes the result,** except on a ballot, when the chair always votes (see [Ways to vote](voting-methods.md)).
- **Can't close debate alone,** and can't rush the vote. Closing debate takes a two-thirds vote. A vote hurried through while someone was rising to speak is void.
- **Motions about the chair** are put to a vote by someone else, like the vice chairman, the secretary, or the person who made the motion. That applies to motions that compliment or criticize the chair, but not to a motion to appoint delegates or a committee that includes the chair.

## Robert's hints for a new chair

- Keep the constitution, bylaws, and rules of order beside you, and know them.
- Know the business coming up, and have a list of the committees.
- Don't let anyone speak on a motion until it's seconded and you've stated it.
- **Don't just rule a motion out of order.** Suggest the right one. If someone moves to "table it until 3 p.m.," ask whether they mean to postpone to 3 p.m. If someone moves to "receive" a report that's already been read, ask whether they mean to adopt it.
- Correct your own mistakes as soon as someone points them out.
- "Never get excited." Never be unfair to the most troublesome member, and never take advantage of someone's not knowing the rules.
- "Know all about parliamentary law, but do not try to show off your knowledge." Be no more technical than the meeting needs. A small, peaceable group may be hindered by strictness. A large or divided one needs it.

Robert's sums up the spirit of the rules with a quotation: "The great purpose of all rules and forms is to subserve the will of the assembly rather than to restrain it; to facilitate, and not to obstruct, the expression of their deliberative sense."

## When the chair is away

- **The vice chairman presides.**
- **If there isn't one there,** the secretary calls the meeting to order and the group elects a chairman pro tem. That person serves until the meeting ends, or until a regular officer arrives.
- **The chair can't hand off.** The regular chair can't name a stand-in ahead of time for a meeting they'll miss.

## At MBC

**Church business meetings.** The Senior Pastor presides at all meetings of the church, except as otherwise provided (Bylaws Art. II.A). He's the moderator of business meetings. If he's absent or disqualified, the Chairman of Deacons presides, then the Vice-Chairman. If none of the three is available or willing to serve, the members present elect a moderator by majority vote (Art. II.D).

**Deacon Board meetings.** The Board elects a Chairman, Vice-Chairman, and Secretary each year by simple majority (Art. II.B §3 ¶3). The bylaws don't separately say who presides over Board meetings. In practice, under Robert's, that falls to the Chairman. Since the Board is small, it can run with the lighter formality Robert's allows small boards (see [Small boards](small-boards.md)).
