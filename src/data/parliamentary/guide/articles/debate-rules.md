# Debate — who speaks, how often, and how long

**Short answer:** Each member can speak twice on the same motion in the same day, up to ten minutes each time. Nobody gets a second turn while someone who hasn't spoken yet wants the floor. Discussion has to stay on whatever motion is in front of the group right now.

## How it works

**Twice, ten minutes, same day.** Those are Robert's default limits. Robert's adds that the limits should fit the circumstances, and that two ten-minute speeches usually work for ordinary groups. To give someone more time, the group needs a two-thirds vote, with no discussion. It's usually handled by general consent. To change the limits for everyone, see [Limit or extend discussion](limit-debate.md).

**New motion, fresh turns.** An amendment or any other motion is treated as a new question. Your two turns on the main motion don't count against you on the amendment. While an amendment is pending, the discussion has to stay on the amendment, unless deciding the amendment more or less decides the main motion too.

**Questions and suggestions aren't speeches.** Asking a question or making a brief suggestion doesn't use up one of your turns.

**The mover can close.** The person who made the motion can give the last speech after everyone else who wants to speak has spoken, as long as they haven't already used up their time. They lose that chance once discussion is closed.

**The mover can't speak against their own motion.** They can vote against it, though.

**The chair can't rush the vote.** If the chair asks, "Are you ready for the question?" and someone reasonably quickly asks for the floor, they're entitled to it. That's true even if the chair has already taken and announced the vote, and the motion goes back to where it was. But if the chair gave people a fair chance and nobody spoke up, it's too late once voting has started.

## Which motions can be discussed?

Every main motion can be discussed. For other motions, Robert's follows a few principles:

- **Urgent, high-ranking motions can't be discussed.** Adjourning, recess, and calling for the agenda are examples. The exceptions are questions of privilege and most appeals, which can be discussed because they usually can't be decided well without it.
- **Motions that set aside a rule can't be discussed.** That includes suspending the rules, calling the question, and limiting discussion.
- **Amending or reconsidering follows the motion it applies to.** If you couldn't discuss the original motion, you can't discuss the amendment or the reconsideration either.
- **Motions that act on another motion allow as much discussion as they interfere with the group's control over it.**
  - *Postponing indefinitely* kills the motion for the session, so the whole motion is open for discussion.
  - *Referring it* only delays it, so the discussion stays on whether to refer it.
  - *Postponing to a set time* allows discussion only of the timing.
  - *Tabling* leaves the group free to take it back up, so there's no discussion at all.

For any single motion, check the [cheat sheet](motion-cheat-sheet.md).

**Even when a motion can't be discussed,** the chair can allow a quick question or explanation before the vote if it'll help the group. The chair should keep it short.

## Watch out for

- **People shouting "Question!"** That's a request, not a ruling. It doesn't end discussion. Only a two-thirds vote can do that (see [Call the question](previous-question.md)).
- **Discussing the main motion while an amendment's pending.** Bring it back to the amendment.
- **Members talking to each other.** All remarks go to the chair (see [Keeping it civil and on point](decorum.md)).
