# Keeping it civil and on point — decorum in debate

**Short answer:** Speak to the chair, stay on the motion, and talk about the measure, not the people. You can criticize a proposal as strongly as you like, but you can't question another member's motives. Robert's puts it this way: "It is not the man, but the measure, that is the subject of debate."

## The rules

- **Stay on the question.** Talk about the motion that's on the floor, not the one before it or the one you wish were there.
- **Address the chair, not each other.** Even a question for another member goes through the chair.
- **Leave names out where you can.** Refer to "the member who just spoke," or something similar, rather than using their name. Refer to officers by their titles.
- **Don't attack motives.** You can say a proposal is unwise, costly, or harmful. You can't say the person who made it is acting in bad faith.
- **Don't criticize what the group has already decided.** The exception is when you're speaking on a motion to rescind it, or you plan to finish your remarks with one.
- **Don't distract.** While someone's speaking, while the chair's speaking, or while a vote is being taken, nobody should whisper, walk across the room, or otherwise disturb the meeting.

## When someone's out of line

1. **The chair calls them to order.** When the chair rises to speak, the member who's speaking sits down until the chair's been heard. If the chair calls a member to order, the member sits until the question is settled.
2. **If their remarks are ruled out of order,** they can't continue if anyone objects, unless the group votes to let them. There's no discussion on that vote, and it takes a majority.
3. **Disorderly words can be written down.** The member who objects, or the secretary, writes down the words and reads them back. If the speaker denies saying them, the group votes on whether they did.
4. **If the speaker can't justify the words and won't apologize,** the group has to deal with it. If the words were personal, each side is heard, and then both people step out while the group decides. Robert's general rule is that no one stays in the room while a matter about themselves is being debated. The member who objected doesn't have to leave unless they're personally involved.

## Watch out for

- **Waiting too long.** If any business has happened since the remarks were made, it's too late to take them up.
- **Remarks aimed at the chair.** Disorderly words aimed at the presiding officer, or about an officer's official acts, don't require that officer to leave the room.
- **Confusing strong with personal.** Robert's explicitly allows a measure to be "condemned in strong terms." Firm disagreement isn't a breach of decorum. Making it about the person is.
