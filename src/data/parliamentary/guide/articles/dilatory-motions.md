# Stalling and nuisance motions — "Dilatory, Absurd, or Frivolous Motions"

**Short answer:** When it's clear someone is using the rules just to keep the group from doing business, the chair can refuse to recognize them or rule their motions out of order. Every group has that right, even without a written rule. The chair should use it only when the obstruction is obvious, never just to move faster.

## What it looks like

Robert's describes a small minority that, by playing within the rules, could stop a meeting cold:

- raising point of order after point of order
- appealing every ruling
- calling for a division on votes that were nearly unanimous
- moving again and again to table or adjourn
- offering absurd or frivolous amendments

His example of a frivolous motion: a speaker will be in town next week, and the group meets weekly. Someone moves to refer the invitation to a committee to report at the next meeting, which would be after the speaker has gone. The chair should rule it out of order.

## How the chair handles it

1. **Decline to recognize the member,** or rule the motion out of order as dilatory.
2. **If they appeal, take the appeal.** Once the group has upheld the chair, the chair doesn't have to take more appeals from the same members while they're plainly obstructing.
3. **Stay courteous and fair, but firm.** Robert's says the chair should protect the group "even though it be done in strict conformity with all parliamentary rules except this one."

## Watch out for

- **Treating honest opposition as obstruction.** Robert's is clear that the chair should never use this "merely to expedite business, when the opposition is not factious." Disagreement, even persistent disagreement, isn't stalling.
- **Rushing the vote.** The flip side is that the chair can't shut out legitimate motions by hurrying. If a member was rising to speak and the chair hurriedly took and announced the vote, the vote is void and the member must be recognized.
