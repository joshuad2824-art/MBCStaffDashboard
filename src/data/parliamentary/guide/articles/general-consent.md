# General consent — skipping the formal vote

**Short answer:** When nobody's opposed, the chair can skip the motion and the vote by saying, "If there's no objection…" and, when no one objects, announcing that it's done. One objection is all it takes to require a regular vote. It's the quickest tool a chair has, and it fits most routine business.

**What to say (chair):** "If there's no objection, we'll [action]." Pause. "Hearing none, [it's done]."

## How it works

**The rules mostly exist to protect the minority.** When there's no minority, there's not much for the rules to protect, so the group can move faster. That's Robert's reasoning for general consent.

**Silence isn't the same as agreement.** General consent doesn't mean everyone's in favor. It means nobody thinks it's worth objecting.

**Approving the minutes is the classic case.** The chair asks, "Are there any corrections to the minutes?" Corrections are made as they're offered. When there are no more, the chair says, "There being no further corrections, the minutes stand approved."

**It works for lots of small things,** like:

- giving a speaker a couple more minutes
- letting a member withdraw a motion
- taking an item out of order
- receiving a report

**An objection undoes it.** If someone objects reasonably quickly, the chair sets aside what was announced, even after announcing it, and takes a regular vote.

## Where it can't be used

- **Rules that protect absent members.** If a rule requires advance notice for a motion, like a bylaw amendment, general consent can't waive that notice. Neither can a unanimous vote. The people who'd object aren't in the room to object.
- **Anything that has to be by ballot.** If the rules require a secret ballot, general consent can't replace it, because nobody can object without revealing how they'd vote. For the same reason, it's out of order to have one person cast the ballot for the whole group.
- **Without a quorum.** See [Quorum](quorum.md).

## Watch out for

- **"Make it unanimous."** After a contested election that wasn't by ballot, someone sometimes moves to make the vote unanimous. Robert's says the only person who should make that motion is the runner-up or someone speaking for them, and that even then it's doubtful. A single no vote defeats it. If the election was by ballot, the motion has to be voted on by ballot too.
- **Using it on something that's actually contested.** If the chair suspects there's opposition, they should just take the vote.

## At MBC

These MBC votes have to be by secret ballot, so general consent can't replace them:

- **Calling a Senior Pastor**, which takes three-fourths of those voting (Bylaws Art. II.A)
- **Approving associate pastoral positions** (Art. II.F)

**Bylaw amendments** require written notice and discussion at a business meeting at least a month ahead (Art. V). That notice protects members who aren't present, so it can't be waived by consent.

**When the Deacon Board chooses absentee balloting for an issue under Policy B001,** everyone's vote on that issue has to be by secret ballot, so general consent is out there too. The issue also can't change once ballots are available, and there's no further discussion of it after the absentee ballots are issued (B001 ¶¶1–2).
