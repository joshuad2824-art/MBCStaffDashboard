# Objecting to even discussing it — "Objection to the Consideration of a Question"

**Short answer:** If a new motion is something the group shouldn't spend time on at all, any member can object to considering it. They have to do it right away, before any discussion. If two-thirds vote against considering it, it's thrown out for the rest of the meeting.

`Second: No · Debate: No · Amend: No · Vote: Two-thirds against considering it · Interrupt a speaker: Yes`

**What to say:** "Mr. Chairman, I object to the consideration of the question." You can say it even if someone else has the floor.

## How it works

**Timing is everything.** It has to be raised right after the motion is stated, before anyone discusses it and before any other motion is made on it. Once discussion starts, it's too late, and the tool for that point is [Call the question](previous-question.md).

**The chair puts it immediately:** "The consideration of the question has been objected to. Will the assembly consider it?" Robert's says the chair can also raise the question on their own if they think it's wise.

**It takes two-thirds voting *no* to sustain the objection.** If two-thirds vote against considering it, the motion is dismissed for the session. Anything less, and discussion goes ahead as if no one had objected.

**It can come back.** A motion thrown out this way can be introduced again at a later session.

**It only applies to new business.** It works against original main motions, along with petitions and communications that don't come from a body above the group. It doesn't apply to motions that deal with the group's existing business. That includes amendments to the bylaws and reports from committees on matters that were sent to them.

## What it's for

Robert's says the point is to let the group avoid a question it considers irrelevant, unprofitable, or contentious. It isn't meant for cutting off discussion. And if the motion is entirely outside what the group exists to do, the chair can just rule it out of order instead, and that ruling can be appealed.

## Watch out for

- **Confusing it with "I object" to general consent.** When the chair asks, "Is there any objection?" one "I object" means a regular vote is needed. That's a different thing (see [General consent](general-consent.md)).
- **Using tabling instead.** Tabling something to avoid it is the unfair shortcut Robert's warns about. This is the legitimate way to keep an objectionable item off the floor, and it requires two-thirds (see [Lay on the table](lay-on-the-table.md)).
- **Waiting.** Once the mover starts explaining, if no one objected first, it's too late.
