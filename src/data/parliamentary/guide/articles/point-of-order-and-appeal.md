# Point of order, and appealing the chair's ruling

**Short answer:** If you think a rule's being broken, say "Point of order," and the chair rules on it right away. If you disagree with the ruling, you can appeal, and the group decides by majority. A tie upholds the chair.

## Point of order

`Second: No · Debate: No · Amend: No · Vote: None — the chair rules · Interrupt a speaker: Yes`

**What to say:** "Mr. Chairman, I rise to a point of order." When the chair asks, state it briefly: "The amendment isn't related to the motion."

**How it works:**

- **It interrupts,** even a speech or the reading of a report. The speaker sits down. You state your point and sit down too.
- **The chair rules,** and can give reasons without leaving the chair. Before ruling, the chair can ask experienced members for advice, which they should give seated so it doesn't look like debate.
- **If the chair isn't sure,** they can put it to the group: "The chair is in doubt and submits the question. Is the amendment germane to the motion?" That vote is final. There's no appeal from it.
- **If the point is upheld and the speaker wasn't seriously out of line,** they pick up where they left off. If their remarks were ruled improper and anyone objects, they need a vote to continue.

**Raise it right away.** A point of order has to be made when the breach happens. Once a motion has been discussed, it's too late to object that it was out of order. **The exception** is a motion that violates the law, the constitution, the bylaws, the standing rules, or basic parliamentary principles. Since it would be void if adopted, it's never too late to raise a point of order against it.

**Not sure if it's a breach?** Ask first: "I rise to a parliamentary inquiry" (see [Withdraw and requests](withdraw-and-requests.md)).

## Appeal

`Second: Yes · Debate: Usually (see below) · Amend: No · Vote: Majority to reverse; a tie upholds the chair · Interrupt a speaker: Yes`

**What to say:** Right after the ruling: "Mr. Chairman, I appeal from the decision of the chair." Any other member seconds it.

**How it works:**

- **The chair states the question:** "Shall the decision of the chair be sustained?" The chair can explain the reasons for the ruling without leaving the chair. "Aye" upholds the ruling, and "no" reverses it.
- **When discussion is allowed,** each member speaks once. The chair can answer the arguments at the end.
- **There's no discussion** when the appeal is about rude or out-of-line conduct, the rules of speaking, which business comes first, or when it's made during a counted vote or while a motion that can't be discussed is pending.
- **A tie upholds the chair,** even if the chair's own vote made the tie. The ruling stands until a majority reverses it.
- **The vote on an appeal can be [reconsidered](reconsider.md).**

**Timing.** You have to appeal right when the ruling is made. If any discussion or business happens first, it's too late.

**What you can't appeal:**

- the chair's answer to a parliamentary inquiry, which is information, not a ruling
- the announced result of a vote. If you doubt it, call for a [Division](division-of-the-assembly.md).
- a ruling made while another appeal is pending. That kind of ruling can be brought up later, when nothing else is pending.

## Watch out for

- **Points of order as a stalling tactic.** The chair can refuse repeated, obviously obstructive points and appeals (see [Stalling and nuisance motions](dilatory-motions.md)).
- **Points of order about tone.** For improper language, it's usual for the chair to call the speaker to order, or for a member to say, "I call the member to order" (see [Decorum](decorum.md)).

## At MBC

The Senior Pastor moderates church business meetings, with the Chairman of Deacons and then the Vice-Chairman presiding in his absence (Bylaws Art. II.D). Points of order and appeals at business meetings go to whoever's presiding. Because Robert's is a general guide "except for changes specifically identified by these by-laws" (Art. IV §2 ¶1), a point of order that a motion conflicts with the bylaws is the "never too late" kind.
