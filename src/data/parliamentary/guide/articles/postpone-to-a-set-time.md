# Putting it off to a set time — "Postpone Definitely" and special orders

**Short answer:** Postponing moves a motion to a specific later time, usually the next meeting, where it comes up ahead of new business. It takes a majority. If you want to guarantee it gets taken up at that time even if it means interrupting other business, make it a *special order*, which takes two-thirds.

`Second: Yes · Debate: Yes, but only on whether to postpone · Amend: Yes (the time, or to make it a special order) · Vote: Majority (two-thirds for a special order) · Interrupt a speaker: No`

**What to say:**

- "I move to postpone the motion to the next meeting."
- "I move to postpone the motion until after the Finance report."
- "I move to postpone the motion to the next meeting and make it a special order."

## How it works

**It becomes scheduled business.** A postponed motion becomes an "order of the day" for the time it was postponed to. If it's postponed to the next meeting, it comes up under unfinished business, ahead of new business (see [Order of business](order-of-business.md)). If that meeting runs out of time before reaching it, it carries over as unfinished business.

**The discussion stays narrow.** People can say why now's a bad time or why later's better. They shouldn't argue the merits of the motion itself beyond what's needed to decide that.

**It can't be taken up early.** The only ways to bring it up before the set time are to [reconsider](reconsider.md) the vote to postpone, or to [suspend the rules](suspend-the-rules.md), which takes two-thirds.

**General order vs. special order:**

- **General order.** A simple postponement. It can't interrupt anything. When its time comes, it gets taken up as soon as whatever's on the floor is finished.
- **Special order.** It sets aside the rules that would otherwise get in its way, which is why it takes two-thirds. When its time comes, it can interrupt the pending business. The exceptions are motions about adjourning or taking a recess, questions of privilege, and special orders made earlier.

**How far you can push it.** You can only postpone something to a time within the current session or the next one. To give it more time than that, the group would first have to set an adjourned meeting and then postpone to it.

## Watch out for

- **A postponement that's really a rejection.** Robert's example is moving to postpone until tomorrow a motion to accept an invitation to tonight's banquet. If postponing would effectively kill the motion, it's out of order as a postponement. The honest motion is [Postpone indefinitely](postpone-indefinitely.md).
- **Postponing a whole category, like "all the reports."** Only the motion on the floor can be postponed.
- **Postponing something the bylaws schedule for a specific meeting.** You can't postpone it in advance. When that meeting arrives, the group can postpone it to an adjourned meeting, for example if attendance is so thin there's barely a quorum.
- **"Table it until next month."** That's a postponement, not tabling, and the chair should treat it as one (see [Lay on the table](lay-on-the-table.md)).

## At MBC

- **"The next meeting" means different things for different bodies.** For the church, it's the next quarterly business meeting (Bylaws Art. IV §2 ¶1). For the Deacon Board, it's next month (Art. II.B §3 ¶5).
- **Deacon elections are scheduled by the bylaws.** They happen at the business meeting preceding the September Deacon Board meeting (Art. II.B §3 ¶9), so they can't be postponed ahead of time.
- **The budget runs on a calendar in Policy A009.** It goes to the Board on November 15 and to the church on December 15, and approval must be complete by December 31. Postponing a budget item has to account for those dates.
