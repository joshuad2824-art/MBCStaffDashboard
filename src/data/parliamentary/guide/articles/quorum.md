# Quorum — do we have enough people to act?

**Short answer:** A quorum is the smallest number of members who have to be present for the group to do business. It counts who's in the room, not who votes. Without one, the group can only adjourn, take a recess, set a time to meet again, or take steps to get more people there.

## How it works

**Usually the bylaws set the quorum.** When they don't, Robert's falls back on a default, and the default depends on what kind of body it is:

- **A body with a fixed, enrolled membership:** a majority of all its members.
- **A church congregation with no quorum in its bylaws:** whoever attends, as long as it's a regular meeting or one that was properly called. Robert's makes this exception for religious bodies where membership is for life and there are no dues, because the membership roll isn't a reliable count of who's actually active, and getting a majority of it to a business meeting usually isn't possible.
- **Committees and boards:** a majority of their members, unless the body that created them says otherwise. A board or committee can't set its own quorum; that has to come from the bylaws or from the body above it.

**No quorum, no business.** Without a quorum, the only things the group can do are:

- try to get a quorum
- set a time to meet again
- adjourn
- take a recess

The group can't act by general consent without a quorum, and notice given at a meeting without a quorum doesn't count.

**The chair shouldn't open the meeting until there's a quorum,** or until it's clear there won't be one.

**Losing it mid-meeting.** You can't interrupt someone who's speaking to say there's no quorum. Discussion can keep going until someone raises it while nobody has the floor. Once it's raised and confirmed, the group can't decide anything else.

**Having a quorum isn't always enough.** Robert's adds that it's usually unwise to settle important business at a thinly attended meeting unless people were told ahead of time that it was coming.

## Watch out for

- **Counting votes instead of people.** If 12 are present and 4 abstain, the quorum is still based on the 12.
- **Changing a quorum rule.** Don't strike the old rule and then vote on a new one. In the gap, the quorum becomes a majority of all members, which may make it impossible to adopt the new rule. Replace it in one motion: strike out the old wording and insert the new.

## At MBC

**Church business meetings.** The bylaws don't set a quorum, so Robert's church default applies: the quorum is whoever attends a properly called meeting. That makes notice the thing that legitimizes the meeting. A regular business meeting is quarterly. It has to be announced from the pulpit during the regular worship service and printed in the bulletin at least two days before (Bylaws Art. IV §2 ¶1). A special called meeting needs the same notice, and the notice has to state the purpose of the meeting (Art. IV §2 ¶4).

**Deacon Board meetings.** The bylaws don't set a quorum for the Board either. Robert's treats a board like a committee, so the default is a majority of the Board's members. The Board is the twelve deacons elected for the current year (Art. II.B §3 ¶1), which makes that seven. This is a reading of the Robert's default. The Board can't fix its own quorum. If the church wants a different number, that would need to go in the bylaws.

**Don't confuse this with the attendance rule.** The requirement that each elected deacon attend three-fourths of the Board's meetings each year (Art. II.B §3 ¶12) is about individual attendance. It isn't a quorum.
