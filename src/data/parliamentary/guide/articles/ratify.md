# Approving something after the fact — "Ratify"

**Short answer:** Ratifying confirms an action that an officer, committee, or delegate took without the group's approval, and makes it the group's own action. The group can only ratify what it could have authorized in advance. It's a main motion and takes a majority.

`Second: Yes · Debate: Yes, and the whole action is open · Amend: Yes · Vote: Majority · Interrupt a speaker: No`

**What to say:** "I move that we ratify the chairman's action on [date] in [action]."

## How it works

**It covers actions the group had the power to approve.** A typical case is something done between meetings that needed approval and couldn't wait.

**Emergencies without a quorum.** The group can also ratify emergency action taken at a meeting that didn't have a quorum. That's true even when the quorum is set in a bylaw.

**It can turn into censure.** When an officer or representative took the action, the motion to ratify can be amended into a motion of censure, and a motion of censure can be amended into one to ratify.

**The whole action is open for discussion.** Members can discuss whether it was wise, not just whether to approve it.

## What can't be ratified

- **Anything that broke the law, the constitution, or the bylaws.** The one exception is the emergency-without-a-quorum case above.
- **A voice vote where the bylaws require a ballot.** Ratifying it afterward doesn't make it valid.

## At MBC

Several MBC votes have to be by secret ballot, so they can't be fixed by ratifying an open vote afterward. These include:

- calling a Senior Pastor (Bylaws Art. II.A)
- electing associate pastoral staff (Art. II.F)

If one of those was done the wrong way, it has to be redone properly.
