# Taking another vote — "Reconsider"

**Short answer:** Reconsider brings a vote back so the group can decide it again, as if the first vote never happened. It has to be made by someone who voted on the winning side, on the day of the vote or the next day. It takes a majority, even if the original motion needed two-thirds.

`Second: Yes (anyone) · Debate: Yes, if the original motion could be debated, and it opens that motion too · Amend: No · Vote: Majority · Interrupt a speaker: Yes, to make it`

**What to say:** "I move to reconsider the vote on the motion to [subject]." If the main motion has already passed and you want to revisit an amendment, name both: "I move to reconsider the votes on the motion and on the amendment to [change]."

## How it works

**Who can make it:** only someone who voted with the winning side. Anyone can second it.

**When:** the same day as the vote, or the next day. A legal holiday or a recess doesn't count as a day. You can make it while other business is on the floor, even while someone has the floor. You can even make it after the group votes to adjourn, as long as the chair hasn't declared the meeting adjourned yet.

**Making it and taking it up are two different steps.** You can *make* the motion at nearly any moment, just to get it on record in time. The group *takes it up* when the business it applies to would be in order. If it's made while something else is on the floor, the chair says, "The secretary will note it," and goes on. Once the floor is clear, anyone can call it up by saying, "I call up the motion to reconsider the vote on…" Calling it up needs no second and no vote, and it gets priority over new business.

**Making it puts things on hold.** Until the reconsideration is acted on, nothing the original vote required can be carried out.

**If it passes,** the original motion is back in front of the group exactly where it was before the vote, and it's open for discussion and amendment again. Anyone who'd used up their speaking turns on it that day can't speak again. But if it's taken up on a later day, discussion is open fresh.

**If it's never called up,** its effect normally ends with the session. In a group that meets at least quarterly, the effect lasts until the end of the next regular meeting.

## What can't be reconsidered

- **Votes on these motions:** adjourning, taking a recess, tabling, taking from the table, suspending the rules, or another reconsideration.
- **A yes vote on any of these:**
  - adopting or amending bylaws or other rules that require notice
  - electing someone who's been told and hasn't declined
  - reopening nominations
  - proceeding to the orders of the day
- **A no vote on postponing indefinitely.** The same question comes up again when the main motion is voted on.
- **A vote that's already been partly carried out,** or that led to something the group can't undo.
- **A yes vote that works like a contract,** once the other party has been told.
- **A vote to refer to a committee,** once the committee has started work on it. Discharge the committee instead (see [Refer to a committee](refer-to-committee.md)).

**Only once.** If a motion to reconsider fails, it can't be made again unless no one objects. No question can be reconsidered twice unless it was substantially amended after the first reconsideration.

## Reconsider and enter on the minutes

This version protects the whole membership from a thin, unrepresentative meeting.

Say a big decision passes at a lightly attended meeting, and it's clearly against what most members would want. Someone who voted with the winning side moves to "reconsider the vote and have it entered on the minutes." Then:

- Action on the vote is suspended.
- The motion can't be called up that same day, which gives time to let absent members know.
- It can only be made the same day as the vote, and only on a vote that finally settles a main motion: adopting it, rejecting it, or postponing it indefinitely.
- It outranks the plain motion to reconsider, and can even be made after a vote on the plain motion, as long as the result hasn't been announced.

If no one on the winning side is willing or able to move it, someone can instead give notice that they'll move to [rescind](rescind.md) at the next meeting. That way rescinding only takes a majority.

**If it's misused,** say to block something that has to happen before the next regular meeting, the group can vote to adjourn to a set day soon so the reconsideration can be dealt with then.

## In committees

A committee can reconsider a vote no matter how much time has passed, and anyone who didn't vote on the losing side can move it, even someone who was absent for the vote. It takes a majority if everyone who voted on the winning side is present or was told ahead of time, and two-thirds otherwise (see [Committees and boards](committees-and-boards.md)).

## At MBC

- **Church business meetings are quarterly** (Bylaws Art. IV §2 ¶1). A reconsideration moved but not called up at one business meeting can still be called up at the next one.
- **The Deacon Board meets monthly** (Art. II.B §3 ¶5), so for deacons it's the next month's meeting.
- **The "next day" window** rarely matters for a single evening meeting. In practice, the plain motion to reconsider has to be made at the same meeting.
