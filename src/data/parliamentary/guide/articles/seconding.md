# Seconding a motion

**Short answer:** Most motions need a second, which just means one other person thinks it's worth the group's time. Seconding doesn't mean you're for it. If no one seconds, the chair says so, and the group moves on.

**What to say:** "Second," or "I second the motion." You don't need to be recognized first, and in a small meeting you don't need to stand.

## How it works

**The point is to save time.** A second keeps the group from spending time on something only one person wants to talk about.

**If nobody seconds right away,** the chair should ask, "Is there a second?" If there's still none after a fair chance, the chair says, "The motion isn't seconded," and moves on. Nothing else should be brought up until the chair either states the motion, says it wasn't seconded, or rules it out of order.

**The chair can skip the second for routine things** when it's obvious the group's in favor. But if someone raises a point of order that there wasn't a second, the chair has to stop and ask for one.

## These don't need a second

- Raising a question of privilege
- A point of order
- Objecting to considering a motion
- Calling for the group to get back on the agenda
- Calling for a counted vote (Division of the Assembly)
- Asking to split unrelated items into separate votes
- Calling up a motion to reconsider that's already been made
- Filling in blanks (names, dates, amounts)
- Nominations
- Asking permission to withdraw your own motion
- Any kind of question or inquiry

## Watch out for

- **Treating a second as a vote.** The person who seconds can speak and vote against the motion.
- **Seconding a motion that's been reworded.** If the mover changes the motion after you seconded it, you can take your second back.
