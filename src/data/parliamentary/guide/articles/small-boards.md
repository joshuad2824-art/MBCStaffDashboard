# Small boards and informal discussion

**Short answer:** In a board meeting with no more than about a dozen people present, Robert's drops most of the formalities. Nobody needs to stand or wait to be recognized, motions don't need a second, there's no limit on how many times someone can speak, and the chairman can make motions and join the discussion without leaving the chair. Votes are still taken formally.

## What changes in a small board

Robert's says: "In a board meeting where there are not more than about a dozen present… it is not necessary to rise in order to make a motion, nor to wait for recognition by the chair before speaking or making a motion, nor for a motion to have a second; nor is there any limit to the number of speeches, nor does the chairman leave the chair when making a motion or discussing a question." The reason he gives is that "the formalities necessary in order to transact business in a large assembly would hinder business in so small a body."

**What doesn't change:**

- **Every decision is still a motion and a vote,** or general consent.
- **The quorum still applies** (see [Quorum](quorum.md)).
- **The chair still states each question and announces each result** so the minutes are clear.
- **Points of order, appeals, reconsideration, and the other protections are all still there.**

## Informal consideration (larger groups)

A larger group that wants a freer discussion on one question can vote to consider it informally. That takes a majority:

> "I move that the question be considered informally."

- **The motion and its amendments are open to free discussion.** There's no limit on the number of speeches, though nobody speaks twice while someone who hasn't spoken wants to.
- **All votes are still formal.** Only the discussion is loosened.
- **Two-thirds can still limit or close discussion.**
- **It ends on its own** as soon as the main question is disposed of, for now or for good.

## Watch out for

- **Informal doesn't mean unrecorded.** The minutes still record every motion and how it came out.
- **Losing track of the question.** With less structure, the chair has to be more careful to restate exactly what's being decided before each vote.
- **Formality has its place.** Robert's notes that in a group that's divided, or where members keep raising points of order, the fastest and safest course is to enforce the rules strictly. If a board discussion turns contentious, the chair can return to the regular forms.

## At MBC

The Deacon Board has twelve members (Bylaws Art. II.B §3 ¶1). That's right at the "about a dozen" Robert's describes, so Board meetings can run with small-board informality. This is a reading of Robert's applied to our Board, not a written MBC rule. Church business meetings are a full assembly and use the regular rules.
