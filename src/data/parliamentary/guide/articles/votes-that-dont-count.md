# Votes that don't count, even if everyone agrees

**Short answer:** A motion that conflicts with the law, the constitution, or the bylaws is out of order. If it passes anyway, it's void, even if the vote was unanimous. The group can't vote its way around its own governing documents. It has to change them first, by the process they set out.

## How it works

**Higher rules win.** The order runs:

1. law
2. constitution
3. bylaws
4. rules of order
5. standing rules and resolutions

A rule that conflicts with one above it has no authority. Robert's example is a bylaw allowing an article of the constitution to be suspended by general consent. That bylaw would itself be void.

**Robert's defaults apply only where the bylaws are silent.** The general rule that bylaws are amended by two-thirds with notice "is only in force when the by-laws are silent on the subject."

**Rules that protect absent members can't be waived by those present.** If a rule requires advance notice of a motion, the members in the room can't skip it, even unanimously. The absent members haven't agreed.

**Ballot rules can't be waived.** If a vote has to be by ballot, it's out of order to vote openly instead, or to have one person cast the ballot for everyone. Either one defeats the secrecy that protects a single dissenter.

**Conflicts with earlier decisions.** A motion that conflicts with something already adopted at the same session, or with something still pending, is out of order. Reconsider or rescind the earlier action first (see [Main motion](main-motion.md)).

## Watch out for

- **"We all agree, so let's just do it."** Agreement doesn't make an out-of-order action valid. If the bylaws stand in the way, amend the bylaws.
- **Treating a void action as settled.** A void vote doesn't become valid because no one objected at the time.

## At MBC

Our bylaws make Robert's a guide "except for changes specifically identified by these by-laws" (Bylaws Art. IV §2 ¶1). So when a motion runs into the bylaws or an adopted policy, the bylaws or policy control. Some examples:

- **Bylaw amendments.** The notice process in Art. V protects absent members, so a business meeting can't waive it.
- **Absentee ballots.** Policy B001 requires secret ballots when absentee voting is allowed. It also rules out proxy voting and absentee voting on calling or dismissing pastoral staff.
