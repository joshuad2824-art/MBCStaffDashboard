# Ways to vote, and when the chair votes

**Short answer:** Most votes are by voice or show of hands. A standing vote is for anything that needs two-thirds, or when a voice vote isn't clear. A ballot is for when people need to vote privately. A tie means the motion fails. The chair votes on a ballot like everyone else, but otherwise only when their vote would change the result.

## The methods

- **Voice.** "All in favor, say aye. Those opposed, say no." This is the usual method.
- **Show of hands.** Common in small groups, and it can stand in for a standing vote.
- **Standing (rising).** "Those in favor, please stand. Be seated. Those opposed, please stand." Robert's says to use this first for anything needing two-thirds. If the chair still can't tell, they ask each side to stay standing until counted.
- **Ballot.** Written and secret. Robert's says it's especially for receiving members, elections, and discipline, and that bylaws should require it in those cases. If the rules don't require it, the group can order a ballot by majority vote or general consent.
- **Roll call.** Each name is called, each person answers yes, no, or "present," and every answer goes in the minutes. Robert's says it's rarely useful in ordinary societies. It takes a majority to order one.
- **General consent.** No vote at all when nobody objects (see [General consent](general-consent.md)).

## Running a ballot

1. Pass out the ballots, and have people fold them.
2. Tellers collect them. If people come forward to vote, the tellers check names off a list so nobody votes twice.
3. When it looks like everyone's voted, the chair asks, "Have all voted who wish to?" If there's no answer, the chair says, "The polls are closed." A formal motion to close the polls takes two-thirds. Reopening them takes a majority.
4. Tellers count:
   - **Blank papers** don't count at all.
   - **Two filled-in ballots folded together** count as one fraudulent vote, and both are rejected.
   - **Small misspellings** don't matter if the meaning's clear.
   - **Votes for someone ineligible** are reported as illegal votes.
5. The first-named teller reads the report and hands it to the chair without announcing who won. The chair reads it again and declares the result. If nobody has a majority, vote again.
6. The secretary keeps the ballots until it's certain the group won't order a recount. A recount takes a majority.

## When the chair votes

- **On a ballot, the chair always votes.** It has to be before the polls close. After that, it takes the group's permission.
- **Otherwise, the chair votes only when it would change the result.** That can mean breaking a tie, making a tie so the motion fails, or tipping a two-thirds vote one way or the other.
- **The chair can't vote twice.** They can't vote to make a tie and then cast another vote to break it.
- **On an appeal, a tie keeps the chair's ruling.** That holds even if the chair's own vote made the tie.

## Other rules

- **A tie loses.** A motion needs more yes votes than no votes.
- **You can change your vote** until the result is announced. After that, you need the group's permission.
- **You don't have to vote.** But Robert's notes that abstaining has the same effect as voting with the winning side.
- **Personal interest.** You shouldn't vote on something where you have a direct personal or financial stake that the other members don't share. You *can* vote for yourself for an office or committee. You can also vote when the motion affects other members along with you, like a meal for the whole group.
- **Absentee and proxy voting.** Neither is allowed unless the bylaws, the charter, or state law provides for it. Robert's says proxy voting conflicts with the basic equality of members.

## At MBC

- **Secret ballot required:**
  - calling a Senior Pastor, which also takes three-fourths of those voting (Bylaws Art. II.A)
  - approving associate pastoral positions, and electing associate pastoral staff, which takes three-fourths of those present (Art. II.F)
  - terminating a membership, voted on individually (Art. I §4 ¶3)
  - the vote on ordaining a Deacon-in-Training candidate (DIT Program ¶H.D). The DIT text and the bylaws disagree on the threshold; see [Majority vs. two-thirds](majority-and-two-thirds.md).
- **Absentee voting:** The Deacon Board can, by majority vote, allow absentee ballots on an issue under Policy B001. If it does, everyone votes on that issue by secret ballot. Ballots have to be picked up in person or brought by a deacon, never mailed. They're available at least a week before and due at least a day before. Absentee voting isn't allowed for calling or dismissing pastoral staff (B001 ¶¶1–4, 6, 10–11).
- **No proxy voting** (B001 ¶11).
