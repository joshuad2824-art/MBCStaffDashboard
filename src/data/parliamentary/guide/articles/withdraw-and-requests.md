# Withdrawing your motion, asking questions, and other requests

**Short answer:** You can take back or reword your own motion freely until the chair states it to the group. After that, you need the group's permission, which usually just means nobody objects. Questions about procedure or about the facts can be asked at almost any time, and they don't need a second or a vote.

## Withdrawing or changing your motion

**What to say:** "Mr. Chairman, I'd like to withdraw my motion," or "I'd like to change my motion to read…"

- **Before the chair states the motion,** it's still yours. Change it or pull it without asking. If you change it, the person who seconded it can withdraw their second.
- **After the chair states the motion,** it belongs to the group. The chair asks, "Is there any objection?" If there's none, it's withdrawn or changed. If anyone objects, the group votes on whether to allow it, and that takes a majority.
- **You can ask any time before voting starts,** even if the motion has been amended.
- **A withdrawn motion is treated as if it was never made.** Anything that was pending on it, like amendments or a motion to refer, goes away with it.

**No second needed.** Another member can move to grant the request without a second, since the person asking is obviously in favor.

## Asking about procedure — Parliamentary Inquiry

**What to say:** "Mr. Chairman, I rise to a parliamentary inquiry."

You don't need the floor, and if it can't wait, you can ask while someone else is speaking. The chair answers questions about procedure that bear on the business at hand, especially when you need to know the right motion to make or whether to raise a point of order. The chair can hold the answer until the speaker finishes if it can wait. If you think a rule's being broken but you're not sure, ask this way first.

## Asking about the facts — Request for Information

**What to say:** "Mr. Chairman, I rise for information," or "Mr. Chairman, I'd like to ask the speaker a question."

It's handled the same way as a parliamentary inquiry. If your question is for the speaker, the chair asks whether they're willing to be interrupted. Questions and answers go through the chair, not back and forth between members. The time comes out of the speaker's time.

## Reading something aloud

- **Reading as part of your speech.** If anyone objects, you can't read from a paper or book as part of your speech without the group's permission. Short printed quotations are customarily allowed.
- **Papers the group is voting on.** Every member has the right to have a paper read once before having to vote on it, and again if it's been discussed or amended.
- **Members who were out of the room** when a paper was read can't insist it be read again.

## Declining or resigning a duty

- **Declining.** If you're elected or appointed to something you can't or won't do, decline right away. If you weren't there, decline as soon as you hear about it. If you stay silent, you've accepted.
- **Resigning.** You're responsible for the job until your resignation is accepted, or at least until there's been a reasonable chance to accept it. Robert's adds that it's seldom good policy to refuse to accept a resignation.

## Watch out for

- **Using a "point of information" to make a speech.** It's for a question, not an argument.
- **Talking to each other across the room.** Even in a question-and-answer exchange, members speak to the chair, not to each other.
